# One Gain Cannot Tame Harsh Voices

Some voices sound too hard at low volume because harshness is a property of a voice's **spectral shape** (how its energy spreads across frequency), not of its loudness, and the Leveler and Safety Cap change only loudness: each applies one gain to the whole spectrum, or none at all to a stream already under the cap, so a voice's balance reaches the headphones untouched. A high female voice stacks its upper resonances and widely spaced harmonics at **2.5-4.5 kHz**, where the outer ear already adds **15-20 dB** ([Venema](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/common-clinical-encounters-59-but-909)); a deep male voice adds **100-400 Hz** boom and dense presence energy at 1.5-5 kHz; and quiet listening tilts the ear further toward treble. The BS.1770 loudness meter is not at fault on treble, where it tracks the ear within about 3 dB ([ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)); it simply has no handle on shape, so no setting of the existing stages can fix this. The protection that fits is a **cut-only dynamic EQ of two or three bands** that judges each band against the rest of the spectrum, cuts a few decibels at most and releases slowly, placed behind a gentle fixed cut if the headphone or the ear is the real source. It would be built as a `BandGuard` stage of fixed-pole band cuts inside the Safety Cap APO (the audio processing object Windows loads into its audio engine), ahead of the cap, and it keeps the project's DSP rules: a bit-exact copy when idle, zero added latency, no allocation, and a frequency response that never exceeds unity. Three problems stand between this plan and a build: the planned `bands.bin` settings file has **no field for the relative threshold** that both the self-test and the "even when low" complaint call for, the tray app has **no audio-capture code**, so every measuring tool is new, and a per-band ceiling is a **soft cap that holds exactly only at the band centre**. The most useful missing fact is the headphone model, because a headphone resonance would put a static cut ahead of everything else.

## Harshness is spectral shape, so turning the volume down cannot remove it

### A high voice lands on the ear's most sensitive band, a deep voice adds boom and buzz

Two regions carry the complaint. Mixing guides place harshness or "presence" at roughly 1.5-5 kHz, sibilance (the hissing S and SH sounds) at 3-8 kHz and higher for women, and boominess or mud at 100-400 Hz, running to 500 ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/how-to-eq-vocals), [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/managing-sibilance)). The guides disagree at the edges, so read these as search areas, not boundaries. Voice physics explains why the two voice types land in different regions. A voice is a stack of harmonics (multiples of its pitch, the fundamental frequency F0) shaped by formants (vocal-tract resonances, F1 to F4, that make vowels). In the Hillenbrand 1995 vowel measurements ([data](https://github.com/santiagobarreda/hillenbrand_et_al_1995)), which [cause.md](Harsh%20voice%20frequency%20protection/cause.md) averages, men's F0 comes to **131 Hz** and women's to **220 Hz**, and the third and fourth formants sit at 2517 and 3541 Hz for men but at **2866 and 4166 Hz** for women; in the vowel /iy/ ("heed") women's second and third formants average 2761 and 3373 Hz. A woman's formants therefore pile up between 2.5 and 4.5 kHz, where the ear canal and concha (the bowl of the outer ear) peak near **2.7 kHz** with a gain of about 15-20 dB ([Venema](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/common-clinical-encounters-59-but-909)) and where listeners rate sounds most unpleasant, 2-5 kHz ([Newcastle University on Kumar et al.](https://www.ncl.ac.uk/press/articles/archive/2012/10/nastynoiseswhydowerecoilatunpleasantsounds.html)). That gain differs between people above 2 kHz by up to about 20 dB, on a secondary source ([Goode](https://www.orl.uzh.ch/memro/program/3.9_Goode.pdf)), so the same voice can hit one listener far harder than another.

Spacing matters as well as position. The ear resolves sound through auditory filters (frequency windows) about **349 Hz wide at 3 kHz** ([ERB formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_rectangular_bandwidth)). By the notes' computation one such window holds about 3.5 harmonics of a 100 Hz voice, 2.7 of a 131 Hz voice, 1.6 of a 220 Hz voice and 1.2 of a 300 Hz voice. A woman with an F0 of 220-260 Hz therefore sends only the 10th to 14th harmonics (2.2-3.6 kHz) through the region, a few widely spaced lines, two of which straddle the 2.7 kHz peak at F0 220 (2640 and 2860 Hz). The notes' hypothesis, which has no direct source, is that a sparse line on the peak can ping, while the dense harmonics of a deep voice beat against each other at F0. That rate sits inside the 15-300 Hz band of envelope fluctuation that psychoacoustics calls roughness, which peaks near 70 Hz ([Fastl and Zwicker](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-68888-4_11), [Salford](https://acoustics.salford.ac.uk/?p=716)), and it reads as buzz.

Deep voices carry a second path, the proximity effect (a close microphone boosts bass). An ideal cardioid microphone in a free field boosts 100 Hz by about **+21 dB at 1 inch, +6 dB at 6 inches and +3 dB at 12 inches**, and by under 1 dB at 24 inches; real microphones read lower ([cmtext](https://cmtext.com/studio/chapter2_mics2.php)). Quiet listening, however, works against bass as the culprit. By the ISO 226 equal-loudness contours, a 100 Hz tone must reach **48.4 dB SPL** to sound as loud as 20.0 dB SPL at 1 kHz at the quiet level of 20 phon (the unit of equal loudness), while 3.15 kHz needs only **14.3 dB** ([IoSR toolbox](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IoSR-Surrey/MatlabToolbox/master/%2Biosr/%2Bauditory/iso226.m), values computed by the notes). The 34 dB spread between 100 Hz and 3.15 kHz at 20 phon narrows to 29 dB at 40 phon (64.4 against 35.6 dB SPL). The quieter the playback, the further the balance tilts toward 2-5 kHz, so "too hard even when low" points more toward 1.5-8 kHz than toward bass, unless a large bass boost sits upstream. ISO 226 holds for pure tones only ([Sottek et al.](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000585.pdf)), which makes these numbers a guide, not a measurement of voices.

### The meter measures loudness well and harshness not at all

The Leveler applies one broadband gain computed from 400 ms of K-weighted loudness (the BS.1770 pre-filter: a head-model shelf of about +4 dB above roughly 1.7 kHz plus a high-pass near 38 Hz), and it leaves a stream under its cap alone. The Safety Cap is a look-ahead true-peak limiter with one gain for all channels. Whatever the spectrum, the plug-in either does nothing or lowers every frequency by the same number of decibels, and a constant shift leaves the shape unchanged. Psychoacoustics states the same point formally. Sharpness (measured in acum under DIN 45692) is normalised by total loudness and "mostly independent of overall loudness"; it weights frequencies above about 15-16 Bark (the ear's critical-band scale, roughly 3 kHz and up) by about `0.066 e^(0.171 z)`, which the notes compute as 1.9 acum at 6 kHz and 2.5 at 8 kHz ([Bader et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08203), [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/sharpness-acum-din45692/)). In the psychoacoustic annoyance model the sharpness term acts only above 1.75 acum, while the roughness and fluctuation term (fluctuation strength is slow modulation near 4 Hz) gains relative weight as the sound gets quieter ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/psychoacoustic-annoyance/)). Both sources are weak (Bader studies music timbre), yet the logic holds: a ratio or a modulation measure does not fall when the gain does.

The meter itself is accurate where loudness is concerned. Against the ISO 226 pure-tone data, the notes compute that K-weighting stays within about **3 dB of the ear at 2.5-4 kHz** (the worst case under-credits 3.15 kHz by 2.6 dB at 20 phon) but over-credits 100 Hz by **26.5 dB at 20 phon, 22.5 at 40, 16.8 at 60 and 10.6 at 80** ([BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx), [IoSR](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IoSR-Surrey/MatlabToolbox/master/%2Biosr/%2Bauditory/iso226.m)). Listeners who matched octave-band noise to a 1 kHz reference at 75 dB(Z) over a loudspeaker agree on where the error sits: the meter read 63 Hz **7.2 dB** and 125 Hz **3.8 dB** louder than they judged it, against about 1 dB at 2, 4 and 8 kHz and 3.18 dB RMS overall ([Fenton and Lee](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/33794/1/AES%20143_AlternativeFilters_FentonLee4_Final.pdf), a manuscript whose abstract alone was peer reviewed). The pure-tone table suggests an 8-18 dB over-credit above 6 kHz, but ISO 226 does not apply to noise-like sibilants and the one 8 kHz noise measurement shows about 1 dB, so the meter does not under-count sibilant energy either. BS.1770 earned its r = 0.977 on 336 broadcast sequences and is "not suitable for pure tones" ([BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)). The Leveler therefore errs toward turning bass-heavy voices down, and a deep voice that sounds too hard at low level is not a case of the meter under-measuring bass. Two voices levelled to the same loudness can still differ strongly in balance, and nothing in the chain can see it.

### Headphone, platform and listener add layers the plug-in can only react to

Three further layers shape what arrives. Headphones concentrate ear-gain energy in 2-9 kHz, and narrow peaks near 8 kHz add grit to S, F and T ([headphones.com](https://headphones.com/pages/measurements-and-frequency-response)). The 2018 Harman target, preferred by 64% of listeners, lifts bass below 150 Hz, while a 21% "less bass" group skews female and older and the "more bass" group skews male 4:1 ([Olive](https://acousticstoday.org/issues/2022AT/Spring2022/files/basic-html/page66.html), [SoundGuys](https://www.soundguys.com/harman-research-targets-the-bruel-kjaer-5128-112247)). An onboard output of about 75 ohms shifts bass over midrange by 2.94 dB on a 100 ohm headphone, against 0.06 dB from a 1 ohm source ([headphones.com](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/the-most-important-spec-you-re-not-paying-attention-to-output-impedance-explained-11)). Platforms process speech before Windows sees it: Zoom ships with noise suppression and echo cancellation on and "Original sound" off ([Zoom](https://developers.zoom.us/docs/video-sdk/windows/audio/sound-options/)), and YouTube's "Stable volume" is on by default with an unspecified algorithm ([YouTube](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14106294)); how either reshapes speech spectra is plausible but unmeasured. Listener sensitivity is the last layer. About 2% of adults have some hyperacusis (lowered tolerance of ordinary sounds) by a cautious count, and some find particular sounds unpleasant whatever the volume ([Tinnitus UK](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Tinnitus-UK-Hyperacusis-Leaflet.pdf)); hyperacusis is defined at low-to-moderate intensity ([Jahn](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9448410)), and clinic discomfort levels run nearly flat across 0.5-4 kHz ([Sheldrake](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2015.00105/full)). Misophonia responds to a sound's pattern or meaning, not its loudness ([Swedo](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.841816/full)). This is information, not a diagnosis.

The notes rank the likely causes for Ariel by judgment, not measurement. Source-side colouration that differs by talker and the ear's own 2-5 kHz amplification lead (both rated high), headphone shape follows (medium-high, model unknown), then qualities a loudness cap cannot see, platform processing, listener sensitivity (low to medium, and checkable by an audiologist), output-impedance bass lift and, last, K-weighting error. Most of the leading causes sit upstream of the plug-in or in the listener, so the plug-in can react to them but not remove them. No study the notes found measures voice-type-specific discomfort at low level, and none tests K-weighting on speech below about 50 dB SPL, so the chain above is inferred from its parts, not measured whole.

## A cut-only dynamic EQ fits best, behind a gentle fixed cut

The requirement has three parts: lower one frequency region and leave the rest alone, act on that region's imbalance at any listening level, and never boost. A single wideband gain fails the first part by construction, and each familiar alternative fails another ([protection.md](Harsh%20voice%20frequency%20protection/protection.md) surveys them). Fixed equalisers, such as Equalizer APO (a system-wide APO with no documented dynamics, [SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/equalizerapo/)) or an AutoEq headphone-correction curve (which needs the exact headphone model and usually boosts as well as cuts, [AutoEq](https://github.com/jaakkopasanen/AutoEq)), work at every level but stay always on, so they thin every voice and cannot tell a harsh one from a gentle one. De-essers (tools that cut the hiss of S sounds) act only on treble: FabFilter's Pro-DS triggers between 2 and 20 kHz and cannot address a low band ([FabFilter](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/basiccontrols)), Logic's DeEsser 2 puts female sibilance at 5-8 kHz and male at 3-6 kHz ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-ae/102006)), and nearly all off-the-shelf de-essers are a single dynamic-EQ band with attacks under 1 ms and releases near 10 ms ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/techniques-vocal-de-essing)), speeds built for brief hisses, not for sustained harshness or boom. Hearing-protection limiters act at 82-118 dB and respond to level alone ([SATRA](https://www.satra.com/ppe/EN352-8.php), [HSE](https://hse.gov.uk/NOISE/acoustic.htm)), and Apple's headphone limit works at 75-100 dB ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/how-to/reduce-loud-sounds-when-listening-over-headphones/)), so none of them engages at a low volume. The notes found no consumer feature built for this complaint; Apple's Headphone Accommodations (balanced tone, vocal range, brightness) only boost ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/120634)).

The closest precedent comes from hearing care. Eddins et al. built a limiter for loudness hyperacusis with eight channels of infinite:1 limiting that passes quieter sound at unity gain. Its limit starts at 35 dB SPL and rises in 1-2 dB steps until running speech at 50-80 dB SPL is judged "loud, but OK", with a 32 ms attack time constant, a 256 ms release time constant and a transient detector under 1 ms ([Eddins et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11192565)). That is the same family as Ariel's stage (cut-only, unity below threshold), and unlike protective limiters it acts at ordinary speech levels. Ordinary hearing aids supply parameter ranges: 3-16 channels, compression ratios generally below 4 (below 3 for fast-acting systems), attacks of 5-30 ms, fast releases of 50-100 ms and slow releases of 800 ms to 2 s ([Patel and Panahi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7380331)). Commercial dynamic EQs show the same pattern: Pro-Q 4's dynamic bands span -30 to +30 dB with an automatic threshold, and its spectral mode targets harshness ([dynamic EQ](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq), [spectral dynamics](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/spectral-dynamics)).

Two heavier options fit worse. Resonance suppressors in the soothe family reduce only, but soothe3 adds 48 ms of latency in standard mode (2304 samples at 48 kHz), 72 ms in linear phase and none in low-latency mode ([soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3)), and the manuals give no speech settings; Gullfoss boosts as well as cuts and adds about 20 ms ([Gullfoss manual](https://www.soundtheory.com/static/Gullfoss%20Operation%20Manual.pdf), [Sound On Sound review](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/soundtheory-gullfoss-v110)). A limiter driven by ISO 532 specific loudness (loudness per frequency band) has no documented real-time form: the time-varying model updates every 1 ms and real-time use is "difficult", and a neural-network stand-in that runs over 100 times faster and lands within about 0.5 phon remains unproven for this use ([ISAAR](https://proceedings.isaar.eu/index.php/isaarproc/article/view/2019-16)). Sharpness is a diagnostic number, not a control law, and has no counterpart for boom; a cheap proxy such as band-energy ratios captures most of the idea.

The notes rank the options by fit to the complaint: a cut-only dynamic EQ of two or three bands with relative detection first, a gentle static comfort EQ as the baseline second, and a soothe-style suppressor third. Build order reverses the first two. Static comes first, because a headphone or ear cause needs nothing more, and the dynamic layer follows only if a static cut deep enough to help dulls everything else. The ranking flips in three cases: a headphone resonance makes static win, mostly sibilant harshness makes a split-band de-esser first, and boom on only some voices makes the low dynamic band first. The starting set below is the researcher's own synthesis, with no head-to-head listening evidence behind it. The body reference is the 500-2000 Hz region of the same voice; attack is how fast a cut engages once a band runs over its threshold, release is how fast it lets go, and a 2:1 ratio removes half of the excess.

| Band | Edges | Detects | Attack | Release | Ratio | Max cut |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boom (deep voices) | about 150-500 Hz | level against the body reference in the same time frame, with a fixed allowed margin | 20-50 ms | 250-500 ms | 2:1 or softer | 3-6 dB |
| Harshness (high voices) | about 2-5 kHz | level against the same body reference | 5-10 ms | 100-250 ms | 2:1 or softer | 3-6 dB, up to 9 |
| Sibilance (optional) | about 4-8 kHz | against the 2-5 kHz band or the body reference | 1-5 ms | 30-60 ms | 3:1 | 3-6 dB |

Keep the stage to two or three bands, because many fast channels smear spectral contrast, and prefer the slower settings. Add a spectral tilt of about 3 dB per octave to the detector, in the manner of Pro-Q's spectral mode, so that the normal downward slope of speech spectra does not read as imbalance (the notes' reasoning, not a sourced figure), and never reference a band to its own long-term average, which would absorb a persistently harsh voice into the norm. No source gives a speech maximum cut per band: soothe notches reach 60 dB in soothe2 and 40 dB in soothe3, and soothe live calls "roughly up to 9 dB" moderate ([soothe2](https://www.oeksound.com/manuals/soothe2), [soothe3](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3), [soothe live](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe-live)), so the 3-6 dB range is a judgment.

## A BandGuard of fixed-pole band cuts fits inside the Safety Cap

### Subtracting a band-pass keeps an exact copy when idle

The method [implementation.md](Harsh%20voice%20frequency%20protection/implementation.md) recommends subtracts a fraction of one band from the signal: `y = x - c * bandpass(x)`. A band-pass filter isolates a band of centre frequency f0 and sharpness Q, and c, from 0 to just under 1, sets how much of it is removed. The filter's poles (the numbers that fix f0 and Q) never move, so only c changes: nothing can destabilise, and at c = 0 the output equals the input exactly. The notes choose the trapezoidal state-variable filter of Simper and Cytomic ([Cytomic](https://cytomic.com/files/dsp/SvfLinearTrapOptimised2.pdf)) with two states held in double precision. In their model an idle stage returned **all 48,000 samples of a speech-like signal unchanged**, the bit-exact copy that `native\dsp\SpDsp.h` lines 33-35 set as a goal and test T10 Part C checks. An RBJ biquad ([cookbook](https://www.w3.org/TR/audio-eq-cookbook/)) with the same response differed in 42 of 30,000 samples while its state error decayed, and a Linkwitz-Riley (LR4) crossover split with per-band limiters ([Rane Note 160](https://www.ranecommercial.com/legacy/note160.html)) matched in none of 30,000. A short-time Fourier transform (STFT) design adds at least one analysis window of latency and sample-rate-sized buffers. The notes also prove the stage is a pure cut: in a cascade its response never exceeds 1 for any c from 0 to 2, while parallel cuts add, so their c values must total 2 or less (three parallel c = 1 cuts at 1000, 1300 and 1700 Hz boosted by up to +4.86 dB). The same engine with a constant c is a static EQ, and on a high band with fast ballistics it is a de-esser.

### End-of-line placement covers every stream without added latency

Windows runs an APO at one of three points: SFX (stream effects) before the mix, MFX (mode effects) after each mode's mix and EFX (endpoint effects) after the mix of everything ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/audio/audio-processing-object-architecture), [signal processing modes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/audio/audio-signal-processing-modes)). The notes put the BandGuard first in `OnProcess` of the Safety Cap APO, an EFX, ahead of `cap_.Process`. One instance per endpoint then covers every stream, raw ones included and whatever Zoom's stream mode turns out to be, with no new class identifier, no registry change and no added latency: the cap stays at **248 frames at 48 kHz** (5 ms of look-ahead plus 8 frames). A copy per stream inside the Leveler would hear one app at a time, but it reaches only Default-mode streams here (the mode lists are Default only) and multiplies the cost by the number of streams. Both lists place the stage after Realtek's and Sound Research's effects, so it cuts what actually reaches the driver, including any bass or treble they add; a Realtek driver reinstall rewrites the lists and drops the registration (`docs\02\WIN11-BUILD-PLAN.md` lines 64, 119 and 143-145), and `docs\03\INSTALL-REPORT.md` line 47 confirms that Realtek's own effects still run before ours. The price of this placement is masking: a quiet harsh voice mixed with loud music in the same band is judged on the mix, so the cut acts on both.

Two APO rules shape the code. A band cut can raise a sample peak, because its phase change reshapes the waveform; the worst-case sample-peak gain is **+1.6 dB at c 0.25 and Q 0.7, +3.3 dB at c 0.5 and Q 1, +5.6 dB at c 1 and Q 1 and +6.6 dB at c 1 and Q 4**. The existing T22-style test that output is never louder than input sample by sample therefore cannot apply to the new stage, and the cap behind it, whose ceiling is `min(cap + 12, -1)` dB (-8 dBFS at the default cap of -20), absorbs the growth. Second, Windows counts failures of `CoCreateInstance`, `IsInputFormatSupported`, `IsOutputFormatSupported` and `LockForProcess`, and at a count of 10 it disables every effect on the endpoint by setting `PKEY_Endpoint_Disable_SysFx` ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/audio/implementing-audio-processing-objects)). A throwing `OnPrepare` surfaces as `E_OUTOFMEMORY` from `LockForProcess`, so the BandGuard keeps its state in fixed arrays and its `Prepare` cannot throw.

### The detector compares each band with a ceiling and smooths in decibels

For each band the detector takes the mean square of the band-pass output over a one-pole window of 5 ms or four periods of f0, whichever is longer, and converts it to decibels relative to a full-scale sine. The gain computer sets the cut to the excess over the band's ceiling, clamped between 0 and a maximum cut, with infinite ratio and a hard knee, and smooths it with the project's dB-domain one-pole, which **snaps to exactly 0** once the cut falls under a small threshold (0.001 dB in the model) and so restores the exact copy. The cut converts to c = 1 - 10^(-GR/20), where GR is the gain reduction in decibels. In the model, with a 3 ms attack and 100 ms release, a 14 dB step reached within 1 dB of the ceiling in about **13 ms**, and recovery to exactly 0 took **963 ms**. The stage has no look-ahead, since the cap's 5 ms look-ahead bounds the peaks. One c serves all channels, driven by the weighted power of the loudest channel, so the stereo image holds and a hard-panned loud voice still triggers. Bands run as a cascade from low to high, each detecting after the earlier cut: a loud 3 kHz tone cut a quiet 6 kHz tone by 6.67 dB through parallel detectors but by only 0.75 dB, the unavoidable skirt, in the cascade.

The cap this produces is soft, not hard. In the model one Q 1.4 band at 3 kHz held a centre tone to within **0.02 dB** of a -20 dB ceiling, but tones off-centre overshot it: **+7.4 dB at 0.85 times the centre frequency, +11.5 dB at 0.7 times and +13.3 dB at half**, with matching figures above (+7.6, +11.4 and +13.4 dB at 1.18, 1.41 and 2 times), because the detector reading falls with distance from the centre and so does the cut. An octave-spaced pair at Q 1.4 left +8.9 dB at the midpoint. On a speech-like signal (F0 220 Hz, formant at 2.9 kHz) the band level fell by only **73% of the 8 dB demanded**, ending 2.2 dB over its ceiling, while harmonics between 2.4 and 3.6 kHz fell 4.2-8.4 dB and everything below 1.5 kHz moved by at most 0.5 dB. More bands, wider overlap or a lower Q shrink the overshoot. The hard guarantees stay with the Leveler's loudness cap and the Safety Cap's peak ceiling.

One design choice stays open: absolute ceilings or relative thresholds. Absolute ceilings (decibels relative to full scale, before the volume slider) match the project's pre-slider design and give a bound that does not depend on other content. A relative threshold (the band's level minus the overall level, compared with R) reshapes the tonal balance and also cuts quiet but spectrally peaky content, which implementation.md names as the candidate fit for "too hard even when low" (untested); the combined rule `GR = max(GR_absolute, GR_relative)` keeps the gain at most 1. The note leaves the choice to the tuning work and says the code only has to carry both parameters, yet its own settings layout and sketch carry just the absolute one, a gap the last section returns to.

### A separate bands.bin, eight telemetry bytes and ten tests complete the wiring

The recommended settings route (Option A) is a new 128-byte `bands.bin` beside `config.bin`, which stays byte-for-byte unchanged: a header (magic "SPBD", version, sequence counter, flags, band count 0 to 4), three shared timing values (attack, release, detector), four bands of centre, Q, ceiling and maximum cut, a snap threshold, 24 reserved bytes and an FNV-1a checksum. An absent or invalid file leaves the stage off, publication reuses the existing sequence lock, the plug-in re-reads settings every 200 ms, and old plug-ins and trays are unaffected. The alternatives are a `config.bin` version 2, atomic with the cap but forcing plug-in-before-tray ordering and touching the golden test vectors, and compiled-in bands switched on by bit 0 of the unchecked reserved word. For telemetry the notes rename the 8 reserved bytes 56..63 of the Safety Cap slot to `int16_t bandGrCentiDb[4]` and keep header version 1, so the tray can show gain reduction per band. The mirror list covers `native\apo\SpShared.h`, `SpRuntime.h` and `.cpp`, `SpSafetyCapApo.cpp`, the stub header `SpDspStub.h`, `src\SoundProtection.Core\` (a bands-file writer and `ApoTelemetry.cs`) and the tests. The installer needs nothing for a tray-written file, though the folder-grant inheritance that should let a second file through follows from the `(OI)(CI)` flags and was not tested.

Ten new DSP tests, T28-T37, cover the bit-exact idle copy, a centre tone capped to within 0.05 dB and within 1 dB in 20 ms, the skirt matching its closed form, no tone ever amplified, energy and peak bounds, non-finite input, chunk-size independence, formats and no allocation, in-place and unprepared use, and disabled and parameter-change cases. Host checks H18-H22 cover telemetry and tone through the real DLL, latency unchanged (H12), a bad `bands.bin` leaving the stage off, safe mode bypassing the stage and many streams. The test table and the `static_assert` at `DspTests.cpp` line 1831 go from 27 to 37, so the existing brief's pin on exactly 27 tests has to change. The tolerances come from synthetic signals and must be re-measured on the real code, since the repository holds no real voice recordings, and "capped" means a tone at the centre sits at the ceiling, not that any tone in the band does.

## Tuning needs capture code the tray does not have

### Every measuring tool is new code

The brief behind the tuning work described a WASAPI loopback sampler (a capture of what the audio engine plays) in the tray app, and [tuning.md](Harsh%20voice%20frequency%20protection/tuning.md) corrects it. The tray plays one fixed 1 kHz sine (`src\SoundProtection.App\TestTone.cs`: 2000 ms, -20 dBFS by default, 50 ms ramps, 48 kHz, shared mode at the user's current volume), reads only master peak, volume and per-app peaks (`WasapiAudioSource.cs`), and polls plug-in telemetry every 100 ms (`Sampler.cs`). A search of every `.cs` file under `src` for Loopback, WasapiCapture, FFT, Spectrum, Goertzel, BiQuad, SignalGenerator and Noise found nothing, and `docs\02\WIN11-BUILD-PLAN.md` line 214 lists process loopback only as a "Later option". Band stimuli, filters, capture and a response interface are all new code.

Loopback in shared mode is the route ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/coreaudio/loopback-recording), [NAudio](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/naudio/NAudio/master/NAudio.Wasapi/WasapiLoopbackCapture.cs)). Microsoft's page does not say whether the tap sits before or after the APOs and the endpoint volume, so a fixed-tone experiment on the real chain has to settle that first; per-process loopback needs Windows 10 build 20348 or later, which Ariel's build 10.0.26200 meets ([Microsoft sample](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/samples/microsoft/windows-classic-samples/applicationloopbackaudio-sample/)). The capture should log band levels, each minus the overall K-weighted level (a level-independent share, because the complaint holds at low volume), the spectral centroid, the eGeMAPS alpha ratio (energy at 50-1000 Hz against 1-5 kHz) and the Hammarberg index (the strongest peak below 2 kHz over the strongest in 2-5 kHz) ([eGeMAPS](https://rdrr.io/github/humlab-speech/superassp/man/eGeMAPS.html), known to the notes through a search summary). Sharpness and specific loudness stay offline with MoSQITo ([code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Eomys/MoSQITo/master/mosqito/sq_metrics/sharpness/sharpness_din/sharpness_din_from_loudness.py)), and installing it is a PC change that needs Ariel's own go. A "that one hurt" button would keep a ring buffer of 10-30 s of feature frames, store matched "fine" frames for comparison and save no audio by default, since call audio holds other people's voices. Because the two sharpness formulas in use differ by about 17% at 18 Bark and 39% at 24 Bark ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/sharpness-acum-din45692/) against MoSQITo), values must be compared within one tool only.

### Two self-tests, run under the cap, set the ceilings and the relative threshold

Audiologists find a loudness discomfort level (LDL, the level at which sound becomes uncomfortable) with an ascending run in 5 dB steps that stops at the first "too loud". Repeat results spread by about **5-6 dB** ([Cox et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004), [Hawley et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28286362)), so finer steps would claim precision the measurement lacks, and Cox warns against reading narrowband results blindly because loudness grows with bandwidth. Standard starting levels can already exceed a sensitive listener's limit: 21% and 24% of 362 patients at two different steps ([Aazh and Moore](https://doi.org/10.1044/2017_AJA-17-0011)). Prescription formulas help less than they seem. NAL-NL2 sets maximum output from thresholds, not measured discomfort ([Keidser and Dillon](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/siemens-expert-series-nal-nl2-11355)), whereas DSL v5 limits speech peaks to the listener's uncomfortable level and sets long-term speech 13 dB below it, because peaks run 10-12 dB over the long-term level ([Scollie](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/dsl-version-v5-0-description-959)); that is the closest template for per-band ceilings. No consumer self-test sets a ceiling: Apple, Mimi and Samsung measure what is audible or preferred and then boost or reshape ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/HT211218), [Mimi](https://mimi.io/blog/how-to-interpret-your-hearing-test-results-mt-test-for-sound-personalization), [SamMobile](https://www.sammobile.com/news/one-ui-tip-get-the-best-audio-quality-adapt-sound/)).

Because the Leveler meters K-weighted loudness, stimulus levels must be stated in K-weighted terms. A sine that reads -20 LUFS has a peak of **-18.6 dBFS at 125 Hz, -20.0 at 1 kHz and -23.3 at 4 kHz** (derived from `native\dsp\SpDsp.h` lines 151-179), so the tray needs a C# copy of the K-weighting or a calibration table. A tone in one ear reads 3 dB lower on the meter than the same digital level in both ears, so one-ear presentations must keep each ear at or under the diotic limit.

Two tests follow, because the complaint does not depend on level. Test 1 is absolute and LDL-style: per band, the lowest K-weighted level called "too hard", T; if "not reached within the cap" holds for every band, the complaint is not about level. Test 2 is relative: real voices (a high woman, a deep man, a mid voice) with one band raised by 3, 6, 9 and 12 dB, renormalised to the same K-weighted loudness, played a few dB under the cap and again 10-15 dB lower; the largest boost still rated "fine" gives the relative threshold R, which is then measured offline with the plug-in's own detector. Run 1 gives T1, run 2 retests only up to 5 dB under T1 and keeps the lower result, and a gap above 6 dB means repeating on another occasion. Catch trials, randomised band order ([Marks](https://auditory.org/asamtgs/asa94mit/4aPP/4aPP14.html)), short bursts instead of continuous tones ([Hellman et al.](https://auditory.org/asamtgs/asa96ind/2pPP/2pPP7.html)) and a fixed anchor guard against fatigue and false alarms. The mapping to the plug-in is direct. The band tested sets `centreHz` and `q`, so stimulus and plug-in share one filter. T minus at least 5 dB sets `ceilingDb`, left unset when not reached. R sets the relative threshold. The excess of hurt-event frames over R sets `maxCutDb`, covering the 90th-95th percentile and no more. No source gives per-person attack, release or detector times, so those stay judgment.

### Safety rails keep every test under the cap

The self-test runs only when telemetry shows the plug-in alive, enabled and out of safe mode, and the output is the protected Headphones. Its top level is the current cap in K-weighted terms and it starts well under it (the notes suggest 30 dB under, a tuning choice). Levels ascend in 5 dB steps, stop at the first "too hard" and never repeat a level that was too hard ([Fultz et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.578352/full)). Bursts are short and ramped, a global Stop key ends the session, a rising slider abandons it, other audio is paused and any trial in which the Leveler reduced gain is invalidated. A plain note first tells the user to stop and see a clinician for any sudden hearing change, which is a medical emergency ([NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness)), and to raise ear pain, one-sided symptoms, tinnitus, ear fullness or dizziness with one; hyperacusis belongs with a GP ([NHS archive](https://tnaqa.mirrorweb.com/ukgwa/20260505142406mp_/https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hyperacusis/)), and one-sided, long-lasting or hearing-affecting tinnitus calls for an audiologic exam ([AAO-HNSF](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25273878)). The test's scale is dBFS through an unknown headphone, so clinical dB HL criteria do not transfer and the test cannot say whether anyone's sensitivity is abnormal. The tray is not a medical device and records nothing that reads as a diagnosis. Whether too much protection harms is an open question, since the NHS advises against constant earplug use or avoiding noise completely.

### Blind listening and log diagnosis prove the stage, within hard statistical limits

Objective checks use a tone ladder at ceiling -10, -5, 0, +5 and +10 dB with existing telemetry, a loopback comparison with the stage on and off, and per-band gain-reduction telemetry. The logs then diagnose the failure: a band cut almost all the time on every voice means a static (headphone or ear) problem; hurt presses with no cut mean the wrong band, ceiling or relative threshold; a large cut with continued hurt means `maxCutDb` is too small or Q too narrow; muffled sound means too much cut; pumping means the release is too short. Listening checks use ABX (is A or B the processed one?) for transparency, 2AFC (two-alternative forced choice) for preference, MUSHRA-style ratings against 7 kHz and 3.5 kHz low-pass anchors for clarity cost ([MUSHRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUSHRA), whose fit to tonal-balance changes the notes did not check), and N-of-1 blocks (one person, repeated blocks) with the COSI questionnaire ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/n-of-1-study-comparative-studies)). The notes' exact one-sided binomial thresholds at 0.05 for 2AFC and ABX are 9 of 10 (p = 0.0107), 10 of 12, 12 of 16, 15 of 20, 17 of 24 and 20 of 30. Power is low: with 16 trials needing 12, a listener who is truly right 70% of the time passes **45%** of the time (80% passes 80%, 90% passes 98%), and with 24 trials needing 17 the figures are 57%, 91% and 99.8%, so one short session cannot show a subtle effect.

Success means fewer "that one hurt" presses and a 2AFC preference for the protected version on the bothersome voices, transparency elsewhere, band levels at or under their ceilings, gain reduction never over `maxCutDb`, no extra clipping and unchanged broadband loudness on fine content. Zero hurt events, an identical-sounding voice and an always-engaged stage all count as failure. Because ceilings repeat only within 5-6 dB, an edit smaller than that cannot be validated by listening. Re-verify after a headphone change, a Realtek driver or effects change, a Windows update or a change in the usual slider position; the Safety Cap's last position in the effect list is neither checked nor repaired (`docs\03\INSTALL-REPORT.md` lines 74 and 313).

## Five gaps shape the build, and six smaller mismatches need decisions

### The relative threshold has no field to live in

tuning.md's second test produces a relative threshold R, the largest boost of a band over the overall level that still sounds fine, and its own mapping table states that "the Option A bands.bin layout has no field for R yet". implementation.md says the code "only has to carry both parameters", yet its layout and its `BandGuard` sketch hold only the absolute `ceilingDb`, and the sketch's gain path has no wideband detector and no relative term. The 24 reserved bytes could hold R, but no note puts it there. "Relative" also carries three meanings across the notes: against a 500-2000 Hz body reference in protection.md, against the wideband level in implementation.md and tuning.md, and against the surrounding spectrum in soothe live. This is the most consequential gap, because an absolute ceiling low enough to catch a quiet harsh voice also cuts every ordinary voice at that level, while a relative threshold judges shape at any level.

### The brief assumed a capture sampler that does not exist

The brief behind the tuning work described a loopback sampler in the tray, and tuning.md corrects it: the tray plays one tone and reads peaks and telemetry, nothing more. That leaves implementation.md's tolerances (measured on synthetic signals) and protection.md's starting values (unvalidated) waiting on a capture tool that still has to be written. Microsoft's loopback documentation does not say whether the tap sees the signal before or after the APOs and the slider, so until a fixed-tone experiment answers that, no captured band level can be read as pre-cut or post-cut.

### Nobody has settled whether "low" means a low slider or a quiet stream

protection.md warns that absolute per-band thresholds are calibrated to sound pressure, while a plug-in ahead of the volume slider sees only digital level. implementation.md keeps ceilings pre-slider on purpose and does not return to the point. tuning.md marks "even when low" as uncertain until Ariel's usual slider position is known. If "low" means a low slider with a loud digital stream, absolute ceilings can still engage, although their acoustic meaning moves with the slider. If it means a quiet stream, only a relative threshold can act. The notes cannot decide between the two readings, and Ariel's slider habit can.

### Ceilings from tone tests overstate protection twice

tuning.md maps a measured T straight to `ceilingDb`, as if the plug-in enforced it. Two softnesses stand in the way. A steady-tone ceiling bites only voices whose band peaks reach it, because the plug-in's short-term detector reads speech nearer its peaks, which run about 10 dB over the long-term level ([Byrne et al.](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/an-international-comparison-of-long-term-average-speech-spectra/), known to the notes through a search summary). And the stage then delivers only about three quarters of the cut it is asked for on speech. The stacking is this report's inference from two notes, not a finding in either. A ceiling meant to catch typical voices must therefore come from the voice test or sit lower than the tone test says, and it needs validation on real voices, of which the repository holds none.

### The deep-voice band is the least supported part of the plan

cause.md finds that bass is least audible when quiet and that the Leveler already over-credits it, so a deep voice that sounds too hard at low level more likely hurts at 1.5-5 kHz presence or in roughness than in the boom region. protection.md's boom band and implementation.md's model bands (250, 800, 3000 and 6000 Hz) nevertheless give bass equal standing, on evidence that amounts to blogs and one manual line. Only the capture and the self-test can settle which band hurts, so the boom band is an option for the first build, not a default.

### What stays unknown

Nothing here has been tested on real voices or on Ariel's headphone, whose model is unknown. Every model-check number in the build section comes from synthetic signals, the `BandGuard` sketch has not been compiled (the build uses `/W4 /WX`), and the CPU figures (about 18 floating-point operations per band, channel and sample, so about 8.8 Mflop/s for four bands and two channels at 48 kHz, plus 192,000 `log10` calls per second) are operation counts, not measurements. Whether a blind on/off switch through `bands.bin` is practical is open, because disabling ramps through the release time and the plug-in re-reads the file every 200 ms. Zoom's stream mode is unknown, and only the end-of-line stage is guaranteed to reach it.

## Conclusion

The notes turn a DSP question into a measurement question. Which band, how deep, how fast and static or dynamic all hinge on facts no note holds: the headphone model, Ariel's usual slider position and the loopback tap point. A headphone resonance, or a cut that engages on every voice alike, would reduce the whole design to a fixed comfort cut, and because the same fixed-pole engine at constant c is that cut, building static first and dynamic second takes one component, not two.

The second implication concerns what "protection" now means. A relative threshold turns the BandGuard from a safety limiter into a tone control that changes how every voice sounds, so transparency on fine content must pass as firmly as protection on bad content. Ceilings repeat only within 5-6 dB, so tuning belongs in coarse 5 dB steps, and ceilings from steady tones are upper bounds, not settings. Finally, no note can say whether the band that hurts on deep voices is bass or presence; that single answer decides whether a boom band belongs in the first build or waits for the capture log.
