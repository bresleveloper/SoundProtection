# Methods and products that protect a listener from harsh treble and boomy bass in voices (cut-only system)

Scope: signal-processing methods and existing products, judged against the brief's constraints: the plug-in may only turn sound down (every gain <= 1), it sits before the Windows volume slider, its current Leveler is one wideband gain driven by a K-weighted 400 ms loudness detector, and the complaint appears "even when low" (below the Leveler's cap). Research date 2026-11-16 (current as of November 2026). Cross-references only: causes, building it inside the plug-in, and per-person tuning belong to the other three researchers.

Flags used below:
- [SNIPPET] = the claim comes from a search-result summary; the cited page was not read directly (blocked, binary, truncated or paywalled), so treat as second-hand.
- [CONFLICT] = sources disagree. [UNCERTAIN] = single weak source, dated source, or unverified.
- "Inferences" are my own reasoning from the cited findings, not sourced facts.

## Key Question 1: Static EQ and headphone correction (AutoEq, Harman target, SoundID Reference): what does it fix, and what does it cost?

### Takeaway
Static EQ and headphone correction act the same at every listening level (and Equalizer APO claims very low CPU use and latency), but a static cut is always on, so it thins every voice and cannot tell a harsh voice from a gentle one. Headphone-correction tools fix the headphone's own response against a preference target (Harman: 64% of listeners agreed, 21% wanted less bass), not the harshness of particular voices.

### Cited Findings
- AutoEq is "Automatic headphone equalization from frequency responses", offered as a web app, Python library, PyPI package and command-line tool; it outputs settings for other equalizer apps ("does not do the equalization itself"), and the CLI flow implies measurement file plus target file in, parametric / convolution / ten-band EQ settings out — [AutoEq on GitHub](https://github.com/jaakkopasanen/AutoEq)
- AutoEq's measurements come from third-party databases (oratory1990, crinacle, Innerfidelity, Rtings) plus legacy headphone.com data; "a headphone missing from AutoEq means it has not been measured by any of the supported sources"; it "treats +10 kHz range as average value instead of trying to fix it precisely" — [AutoEq on GitHub](https://github.com/jaakkopasanen/AutoEq)
- AutoEq's target collection includes two Harman over-ear 2018 files (one "without bass"); the optimizer supports shelf filters (update dated 2022-09-18) and "has limits for Fc, Q and gain value ranges" (the ranges are not stated); the example config sets max gain to 24 and a bass boost of 6 — [AutoEq on GitHub](https://github.com/jaakkopasanen/AutoEq)
- [SNIPPET] AutoEq's wiki reportedly caps precomputed results at 6 dB max gain versus 12 dB in the web app — [AutoEq wiki](https://www.github.com/jaakkopasanen/AutoEq/wiki)
- [CONFLICT, SNIPPET] A fork says stock Harman is AutoEq's default target; the author's Medium article implies a flat 0 dB target when measurements are already compensated; neither was checked against live code — [iwalton3 fork](https://github.com/iwalton3/AutoEq); [Medium article](https://medium.com/@jaakkopasanen/make-your-headphones-sound-supreme-1cbd567832a9)
- Harman target: "a majority (64%)" of listeners from a broad range of ages, experience and genders preferred it; a "Less Bass Is Better" class was 21% of listeners and "includes a disproportionate percentage of females and older listeners" and "none of the trained listeners"; the "More Bass Is Better" class skewed male 4 to 1; the farther a headphone's response strays from the target the lower its predicted rating; the target is "intended as a guideline"; criticisms: limited headphones, programs and female listeners tested, and the effect of hearing loss on preference is unresolved — [Acoustics Today, Spring 2022, p. 66](https://acousticstoday.org/issues/2022AT/Spring2022/files/basic-html/page66.html)
- [SNIPPET] SoundID Reference offers Windows systemwide calibration through a "Windows audio device insert", advertises near-zero latency, and its Custom Target mode offers bell and shelf filters with adjustable Q; no dedicated brightness slider was found, and it is not confirmed that Custom Target edits apply in the Windows systemwide path. The store page itself returned HTTP 429 — [Sonarworks store](https://store.sonarworks.com/products/soundid-reference-for-speakers-headphones); [Sonarworks what's new](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/product-news/soundid-reference-whats-new)
- Equalizer APO is "A system-wide equalizer for Windows 7 / 8 / 8.1 / 10 / 11", implemented as an Audio Processing Object; it claims "very low latency" and "low CPU usage" (no figures), lists "VST plugin support", cannot see apps that use ASIO or WASAPI exclusive mode, is version 1.4.2 (last update 2025-11-28); Peace is a separate GUI project for it — [Equalizer APO on SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/equalizerapo/)
- Equalizer APO filter types include peaking, low/high shelf (also fixed 6 and 12 dB slopes), notch, low/high-pass, band-pass, all-pass and custom IIR; the Stage command sets pre-mix or post-mix, "Post-mix is the better choice. Pre-mix filtering runs separately for each application, which uses more CPU"; "Preamp: <Negative number> dB" is a fixed gain offset that prevents clipping when filters add positive gain; no dynamic or level-dependent processing is documented on this reference page — [Equalizer APO configuration reference](https://sourceforge.net/p/equalizerapo/wiki/Configuration%20reference/)
- The ear is "most sensitive between 2 and 5 kHz", largely due to ear-canal resonance and the middle-ear transfer function; the contours were standardized as ISO 226:2003 — [Wikipedia: Equal-loudness contour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal-loudness_contour)
- Equal-loudness contours show "a notable discrimination against low frequencies for very soft sounds", a response that is "flatter" at loud levels, and a 2000-5000 Hz sensitivity enhancement attributed to ear-canal resonance — [HyperPhysics](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/sound/eqloud.html)

### Inferences
- A linear static filter applies the same dB change at any listening level, so it acts the same "even when low", whereas the Leveler's cap cannot act below the cap (dynamic cuts with relative detection also act at low levels, see Key Question 2).
- The cost is "always on": the cut thins every sound, voices and music that are not harsh included, and the amount of dulling scales with the cut depth. No source quantifies how much static cut in 2-5 kHz or 150-500 Hz listeners accept.
- Headphone-correction filter sets such as AutoEq's typically involve boosts as well as cuts (the README's example config sets a max gain of 24 and a bass boost of 6, with no stated defaults or allowed ranges). A cut-only version would have to shift the whole curve down so no band exceeds 0 dB, as Equalizer APO's negative Preamp does, which lowers overall level that the volume slider must then recover.
- Static EQ suits stable causes (a headphone resonance, a listener's own sensitivity in the 2-5 kHz region where hearing is most sensitive). It cannot distinguish a harsh high voice from a gentle one, so it must be set for a compromise.
- The "less bass" group (disproportionately older and female) shows that no single target fits everyone; this belongs to the per-person tuning researcher. AutoEq needs a known headphone model, and Ariel's is unknown (brief), so AutoEq cannot be applied until the model is identified or measured (cross-reference: per-person tuning and causes researchers).

### Gaps
- No source found that measures how much static cut listeners accept before speech sounds dull or less intelligible.
- SoundID Reference product details came only from search summaries; whether Custom Target works in its Windows systemwide path is unconfirmed.
- AutoEq's current default target and whether it has a cut-only (no-boost) mode were not verified.

## Key Question 2: Cut-only dynamic EQ, multiband compressors and limiters, wideband and split-band de-essers, and spectral or resonance suppressors: which suits harsh high voices, which suits boomy deep voices, what artifacts, what latency?

### Takeaway
Frequency-selective dynamic cuts (dynamic EQ bands, split-band de-essers, spectral or resonance suppressors) fit harsh high voices well (documented bands 2-8 kHz) and can be pointed at a low band for boomy voices, but the evidence for the low band is weaker (blogs, one manual line). Artifacts are lisping (wideband), pumping (slow release), over-smoothing (too fast), phase distortion on drastic cuts, and pre-ringing or tens of milliseconds of latency with linear-phase designs; zero-latency modes exist.

### Cited Findings
#### Dynamic EQ (FabFilter Pro-Q 4 help as the documented reference)
- A dynamic band's gain changes with input level; the dynamic range ring spans "-30 to 30 dB" (positive values expand, negative values compress), only Bell, Shelving and Flat Tilt bands support it [CONFLICT: the page intro says "bell or shelf shapes" while the controls section adds flat tilt, and the page does not reconcile this]; the page never states a cut-only option — [Pro-Q 4: Dynamic EQ](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq)
- The threshold's top slider position is automatic ("A"); in Auto mode (default) attack and release are set automatically and "the threshold keeps adjusting to the level of the band-filtered detection signal"; attack/release knobs centre at 50% = auto; by default the trigger is the input filtered to the band's own range, with a "Free" option adding cut filters; no ratio, time constants or knee values are given and behaviour is "highly program dependent" — [Pro-Q 4: Dynamic EQ](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq)
- Processing modes are Zero Latency, Natural Phase and Linear Phase (no latency figures); Linear Phase supports dynamic EQ only up to Processing Resolution "High" — [Pro-Q 4: Dynamic EQ](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq)
- Spectral mode keeps the band's overall gain fixed and processes only the frequencies inside the band that exceed the threshold; "Spectral Density" sets selectivity; "Spectral Tilt" applies a "3 dB/oct" tilt to the incoming spectrum before triggering (on by default for new spectral bands, nudging triggering toward higher frequencies); recommended for "harshness and problem frequencies in guitar or vocal recordings"; spectral bands require linear-phase processing, "Low or Medium" resolution is favoured above 1000 Hz, and "Very High" and "Maximum" are unavailable for spectral or dynamic EQ — [Pro-Q 4: Spectral Dynamics](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/spectral-dynamics)

#### De-essers: wide band versus split band
- Pro-DS Single Vocal mode uses "a highly intelligent detection algorithm, which splits sibilance from non-sibilance"; Allround mode triggers only on the HP/LP sidechain frequency range and Threshold, and is "intended for processing entire mixes"; the trigger range can be "any range between 2 kHz and 20 kHz"; with Threshold at -INF dB in Single Vocal mode "all sibilance will then be reduced by (roughly) the same amount (specified by Range)"; typical vocal "s" sounds are "around 8 to 10 kHz" — [Pro-DS basic controls](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/basiccontrols)
- Pro-DS Full/Wide Band (the pages use both names for this mode) lowers gain across the whole signal; Split Band reduces only the highs; Wide Band "often works well on single vocals" and in some cases "sounds more natural"; Split Band suits full mixes and keeps the low end untouched; split-band with Allround can act as a "high frequency limiter" followed by a high shelf to restore the top (a boost) — [Pro-DS advanced controls](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/advancedcontrols)
- Pro-DS lookahead is up to 15 ms ("about 10 ms" often optimal for vocals); with lookahead on, latency is 15 ms plus a small extra with oversampling or split band; with lookahead off, processing is Wide Band and "works without any latency"; oversampling "reduces possible aliasing for fast/aggressive de-essing" at CPU cost; the pages do not say whether the threshold is absolute or relative, and give no attack/release times — [Pro-DS advanced controls](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/advancedcontrols); [Pro-DS overview](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/overview)
- iZotope RX De-ess has two algorithms: Classic "attenuates them with a broadband gain envelope"; Spectral is "a multiband compressor with dozens of bands" that acts only on the top of the spectrum and avoids modulating background noise; Threshold has a Relative default (measures speech level and places the threshold accordingly) or Absolute (dBFS); Speed is Fast or Slow; too slow causes "pumping in the high frequencies", too fast adds "a smoothing effect that reduces useful high frequency definition" — [iZotope RX 9 De-ess](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-ess/index.html)
- [CONFLICT, SNIPPET] A search summary says an RX 7 snippet appears to recommend slow mode for the same pumping symptom that the RX 9 page fixes with fast mode, so the advice may vary by version; the RX 7 page itself was not read, nor were Ozone's dynamic EQ manual or newer RX versions' pages — [iZotope RX 7 De-ess](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx700/en/de-ess/index.html)
- Logic Pro DeEsser 2: female sibilance "5-8 kHz", male "3-6 kHz" (general figures); Split (narrow) versus Wide band; Max Reduction knob; Relative mode is "for lower-level signals and a more responsive type of gain reduction", Absolute "for higher-level signals and more classic de-essing" — [Apple Support: Reduce sibilance in Logic Pro](https://support.apple.com/en-ae/102006)
- Sound On Sound (Mike Senior, 2009): simple level-based full-band de-essing "can struggle to control the harshness of a vocal's sibilance without lisping side-effects"; frequency-selective processing gives "a smoother vocal sound with less reduction in the overall sibilance levels" and "potentially fewer lisping side-effects"; sibilance is "usually focused somewhere in a region from 4-10kHz"; detector high-pass "at around 4kHz"; "sub-1ms attack time if possible", release "very short (around 10ms)", longer releases cause "unmusical level lurches"; "Almost all off-the-shelf de-essers" are a single band of dynamic EQ; over-softening makes "the singer sound like they're lisping" — [Sound On Sound: vocal de-essing](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/techniques-vocal-de-essing)
- [SNIPPET] Typical de-ess timing across blogs and manuals: attack about 5-10 ms and release 40-60 ms (Unison), attack below about 5 ms "can squash vocals"; manual ranges are much wider (TC M-One XL attack 0.5-50 ms, release 20 ms-7 s; Steinberg WaveLab release 1-1000 ms) — [Unison: de-essing](https://unison.audio/de-essing/); [Unison: what is a de-esser](https://unison.audio/what-is-a-de-esser/); [TC Electronic M-One XL manual](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/280340/tc-electronic-m-one-xl.html?page=37); [Steinberg deesser r](https://archive.steinberg.help/wavelab_yellowtec_edition/v1/en/_shared/topics/plug_ref/deesser_r.html)

#### Multiband dynamics: phase and latency (FabFilter Pro-MB) and a shipping multiband limiter (Dolby Audio Regulator)
- Dynamic Phase (default): "zero latency operation", "no pre-ringing", avoids "static phase distortion", "minor phase effects will only be introduced when the gain of a band is actually changed"; Linear Phase: "quite a bit of extra latency" and "possible pre-ringing artifacts", "an inevitable side-effect of any kind of linear phase processing"; Minimum Phase: no extra latency but "static phase changes at the crossover frequencies" that become "very apparent and often unpleasant" at higher slopes (6 dB/oct slopes are "very suitable for mastering") — [Pro-MB processing mode](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-mb/using/processingmode)
- Dolby's Audio Regulator (part of the PC Entertainment Experience v4 suite) applies multiband compression so the signal never exceeds per-band limits stored per PC model, to stop the PC speakers distorting: "Signals that are lower than the limits are left alone, and signals approaching the limits are attenuated"; it applies "intelligent constraints" so the timbre is roughly maintained, and its compression is reduced as the PC volume is turned down — [Dolby PC Entertainment Experience v4 white paper](https://professional.dolby.com/siteassets/pdfs/dolby-pc-entertainment-experience-v4-white-paper.pdf)

#### Resonance and spectral suppressors (oeksound soothe family)
- soothe2 is a "dynamic resonance suppressor" that "applies reduction automatically"; the manual describes reduction only (boosting a band on the depth curve means it "reduces resonances more in this area"); Depth gives "up to 60 dB notches" and extreme depth can "mangle the sound source beyond recognition"; Soft mode is "less level-dependent", Hard mode "more level-dependent"; attack and release are frequency-dependent (faster on highs); fast filters on drastic cuts cause "audible phase distortion", fixed by a higher Release; no latency figures, no vocal or speech settings (only de-essing advice: lower Selectivity for wide-spectrum sibilance); Eco resolution "roughly halves the CPU usage" but can add artifacts — [soothe2 manual](https://www.oeksound.com/manuals/soothe2)
- soothe3 (search summaries give a launch date of 19 May 2026 and say it replaced soothe2 as the current release [SNIPPET]): up to 8 bands (including bell, band reject, tilt), a Max cut control, Soft mode with "adaptive threshold" versus Hard mode "level-dependent", notches "up to 40 dB"; latency table at 48 kHz: Standard 2304 samples (48 ms), Low latency 0 samples, Linear phase 3456 samples (72 ms); at 96 kHz Low latency is 96 samples (1 ms) (the sample counts are the manual's; the ms figures are conversions at the stated sample rate); filter curves default to minimum phase; low latency "gives up lookahead" so it reacts later to changes such as vibrato; suggested use includes "harsh or boomy resonances on single instrument and vocal channels"; fast release can sound "breathy" and cause phase distortion on drastic cuts; the word "speech" never appears — [soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3); launch date and "replaced soothe2" wording from search summaries that listed [weraveyou](https://weraveyou.com/2026/05/oeksound-soothe-3-launch/) and [KVR Audio](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=9244645) (those pages were not read)
- [CONFLICT] One review gives soothe3 standard latency as roughly 24-64 samples, against the manual's 2304 samples; the manual is the primary source — [MixingGPT review](https://mixinggpt.com/blog/oeksound-soothe-3-review-2026) versus [soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3)
- soothe live (live-sound adaptation): factory default "optimized for vocals and monophonic instruments", "more level-dependent than Soothe2", it "flags resonant frequencies, then checks each against the surrounding spectrum"; Detail "3-7" suits "vocals and most instruments", Speed "2-5" gives matched attack and release; six bands (high cut, low cut, four bells); depth must be reset per source because it is level-dependent; [CONFLICT] algorithmic latency is "64 samples on both platforms" in the text but the table lists 32 at 44.1/48 kHz and 64 at 88.2/96 kHz — [soothe live manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe-live)

#### Perceptual masking EQ (Gullfoss) as a counter-example
- Gullfoss is not cut-only: "for both Recover and Tame the EQ boosts and cuts different frequency ranges" to preserve perceived loudness; Recover and Tame range 0-200%; Bias and Brighten -100% to +100%; Boost -50 to +50 dB simulates the change in perceived balance with listening level; latency of approximately 20 ms in the spec list and "around 21 milliseconds" in the Live section (Live about 2 ms), about 1000 auditory-model updates per second and about 300 EQ updates per second; "up to four times the load from Best compared to Normal"; frequency range limiters restrict Tame and Recover to chosen ranges; manual is rev 1.3 dated 2021-11-16 (Gullfoss 1.11) [UNCERTAIN currency] — [Gullfoss operation manual](https://www.soundtheory.com/static/Gullfoss%20Operation%20Manual.pdf)
- A review says Tame "deals with the same situation in reverse by de-emphasising dominant elements", that "using Tame just in the sibilance band turns Gullfoss into a brilliant de-esser", that Live "has to incorporate a predictive element" that "may occasionally be less accurate on signals with a rapid rate of change", and that the Standard and Master editions show 21 ms latency — [Sound On Sound: Gullfoss 1.10](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/soundtheory-gullfoss-v110)

### Inferences
- Cut-only is natural for these tools: soothe is reduction-only per its manuals, and a Pro-Q-style band with 0 dB static gain and a negative dynamic range only cuts (negative range = compress per the help page; the page does not say cut-only outright).
- A single wideband gain cannot change spectral balance: it lowers every frequency by the same dB, so the high-to-low ratio of a voice is unchanged. Wide-band de-essing works for brief sibilant bursts (FabFilter calls it natural on single vocals), but sustained harshness or boom, and the existing wideband Leveler, only lower the level. This is why a frequency-selective cut is needed for the complaint (my reasoning).
- Detection mode matters more than band count for low listening levels. Adaptive or relative detectors (Pro-Q's automatic threshold, soothe Soft mode, iZotope Relative, Logic Relative, Pro-DS Single Vocal at -INF dB) are documented as adapting to the signal instead of using a fixed dB level, so they are likely to keep acting at any playback level (my inference; none of the pages states this outright); absolute thresholds depend on level (Logic recommends Absolute for higher-level signals; soothe Hard mode is "more level-dependent"; soothe live needs depth reset per source and is "more level-dependent than Soothe2").
- Relative must mean across frequency within the same moment (a band against neighbouring or reference bands, as soothe live's check of each resonance against the surrounding spectrum does), not against that band's own long-term average; an average-tracking threshold would absorb a persistently harsh voice and stop cutting it. Pro-Q's automatic threshold is documented as adjusting to "the level of the band-filtered detection signal", which on its face is a band-own reference, so whether it would absorb a persistently harsh voice is unknown and needs a listening test [UNCERTAIN].
- Mapping to the complaint: high voices fit bands at about 2-5 kHz (harshness) and 5-8 kHz (sibilance) using a split-band de-esser, dynamic EQ bands or spectral bands; deep voices fit a low-mid band at about 150-500 Hz using a dynamic EQ band or the low band of a multiband. Source quality is high for the high band and low for the low band.
- Latency options: zero latency exists (minimum-phase filters, Pro-MB Dynamic Phase, soothe3 low latency) at the price of no lookahead or static phase shifts; FFT-based and linear-phase designs cost tens of milliseconds (soothe3 Standard 48 ms and Linear phase 72 ms at 48 kHz; Gullfoss about 20-21 ms), and linear phase adds pre-ringing. Audio/video sync tolerance was not researched.
- Spectral suppressors (soothe, Pro-Q spectral) are the most automatic way to handle different voices because they find resonances relative to the surroundings, but they are the heaviest and their manuals give no speech starting values.
- Cross-reference: how to place filters per stream or post-mix and the latency budget belong to the building researcher.

### Gaps
- Pro-DS help pages do not state whether Threshold is absolute or relative, nor give attack and release times.
- No latency figure for soothe2; soothe3 standard-latency conflict and soothe live latency inconsistency are unresolved.
- No documented tool or measured evidence for a de-esser-like control of low-frequency boom in voices was found; iZotope RX De-plosive was not researched.
- No blind-test or measured artifact data was found; artifact claims come from manuals and reviews.
- Waves de-essers (named in the brief) were not read; iZotope current-version docs (RX 12, Ozone 12 Dynamic EQ) were not read.

## Key Question 3: Perceptual approaches (specific loudness per critical band, ISO 532-1/-2, sharpness-driven control): are they practical in real time?

### Takeaway
No documented real-time cut-only limiter built on ISO 532 specific loudness was found; the time-varying Moore-Glasberg model is described as "difficult" in real time, and a neural-network stand-in was proposed. Sharpness (DIN 45692) is a loudness-normalised diagnostic scalar, not a control law, and no product using it for control was found; the practical perceptual tools are simpler (K-weighting, block-wise loudness, per-band levelers).

### Cited Findings
- ISO 532-1:2017 (Zwicker): two methods, stationary and time-varying; published 15 June 2017, reviewed 2022, status stage 90.92 "International Standard to be revised" (2024-11-01) with ISO/DIS 532-1 in development; the page gives no timing figures — [ISO 532-1 page](https://committee.iso.org/standard/63077.html)
- [SNIPPET] ISO 532-2:2017 (Moore-Glasberg) covers steady sounds only; reconfirmed in 2022 but flagged for revision with a second edition (ISO/CD 532-2) in development — [ISO 532-2 page](https://committee.iso.org/standard/63078.html)
- [SNIPPET] ISO 532-3:2023 (Moore-Glasberg-Schlittenlacher) covers steady and time-varying sounds, with a second edition in development and reference source code as informative material — [ISO 532-3 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/69856.html)
- The time-varying loudness (TVL) model "calculates 'instantaneous loudness' every 1 ms" and "real-time implementation of the TVL model is difficult"; a feedforward network with three hidden layers predicts instantaneous loudness typically within 0.5 phons and "over 100 times more quickly"; no absolute real-time figures are given; the paper is Schlittenlacher, Turner and Moore — [ISAAR proceedings](https://proceedings.isaar.eu/index.php/isaarproc/article/view/2019-16)
- [SNIPPET] A search summary says that building the excitation pattern absorbs most of the processing, and that a 2012 AES paper measures the accuracy lost to its time- and frequency-domain shortcuts against the compute saved on real music; that AES page was not read — [AES elib 16511](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=16511)
- Windows Loudness Equalization "simulates human hearing to accurately measure the loudness", uses single-pass processing that works "on a block-by-block basis" with a block that "corresponds to the critical band resolution of a human year" (sic), and adjusts the gain "with a fast attack and slow decay-just as many wideband compressors do-to tightly control the peak loudness of a signal while maintaining the local dynamics"; it also makes "the quiet parts of the content loud enough to be audible", and "is not full" (it preserves some louder-versus-softer sense) — [Microsoft Learn: Loudness Equalization DSP](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-hardware/drivers/audio/loudness-equalization-dsp)
- Dolby Volume Leveler "listens" with "a psychoacoustic model of loudness perception", and can "target its adjustments at individual channels of the audio as well as individual frequency bands to prevent 'pumping' and 'breathing' artifacts"; the white paper carries a "2010 Dolby Laboratories" copyright notice and mentions iTunes, Windows Media Player and Skype, so its feature descriptions date from 2010 [UNCERTAIN currency] — [Dolby white paper](https://professional.dolby.com/siteassets/pdfs/dolby-pc-entertainment-experience-v4-white-paper.pdf)
- [SNIPPET] Seefeldt's "Loudness Domain Signal Processing" (AES paper 7180, 123rd Convention 2007, Dolby) re-expresses perceived loudness on a uniform scale across frequency and time, edits in that domain, and runs the psychoacoustic model in reverse; volume control and automatic leveling are the examples; the full text is paywalled — [AES elibrary](https://aes.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=14238)
- Sharpness per DIN 45692: S = 0.11 x sum(N'(z) g(z) z dz) / sum(N'(z) dz) acum, with g(z) = 1 up to 16 Bark and 0.066 e^(0.171 z) above; 1 acum = narrowband noise centred at 1 kHz at 60 dB; the sum is normalised by total loudness; DIN, Zwicker and Aures variants "are not perfectly interchangeable" — [MetricGate: sharpness](https://metricgate.com/docs/sharpness-acum-din45692/)
- [SNIPPET] DIN 45692:2009-08 is the current edition; the von Bismarck, Aures and DIN methods all work from specific loudness — [DAGA 2017 paper](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/DAGA_2017/data/articles/000299.pdf); [UNCERTAIN] the same search summary says "the Aures method's output depends on absolute loudness" but attributes it to "the same vendor page" without naming it (Weisang and MetricGate were both among the hits) — [Weisang](https://www.weisang.com/en/?p=24503); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/sharpness-acum-din45692/)
- ITU-R BS.1770-5 loudness: a two-stage K-weighting pre-filter (stage 1 models the acoustic effect of the head as a rigid sphere, stage 2 is a high-pass), mean square, channel-weighted sum, then gating of 400 ms blocks overlapping by 75% with thresholds at -70 LKFS and -10 dB relative; coefficient tables are for 48 kHz — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- Gullfoss's manual says it "bases its calculations on the information present in the signal and not some spectral power measure" using an auditory-perception model updated about 1000 times per second — [Gullfoss operation manual](https://www.soundtheory.com/static/Gullfoss%20Operation%20Manual.pdf)
- Unpleasant-sound band: [CONFLICT, SNIPPET] press accounts of the Kumar and Griffiths work put it at roughly 2000-5000 Hz, a 2023 Frontiers paper traces a 2.5-5.5 kHz band (with 1-16 Hz modulation) to a 2008 Kumar et al. study; the primary papers were not read — [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1126481/epub); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/251489.php)

### Inferences
- Limiting specific loudness per critical band is conceptually a ~24-band multiband limiter in a loudness domain, cut-only if implemented as per-band attenuation. It is the most principled way to account for the ear's level-dependent balance (bass discriminated against when soft, 2-5 kHz sensitivity persistent), but no source documents a real-time version, and the standardized models are heavy. Practical status: unproven for this use [UNCERTAIN].
- My calculation from the BS.1770-5 coefficient tables (48 kHz), relative to 1 kHz: -4.6 dB at 50 Hz, -1.8 dB at 100 Hz, about -0.7 to -1.0 dB at 200-500 Hz, +2.4 dB at 2 kHz, +3.1 dB at 3 kHz, about +3.3 dB from 4 kHz to 16 kHz. K-weighting therefore differs from flat by at most about 3 dB in the harshness band and under 2 dB at 100 Hz, so a K-weighted level detector is nearly blind to spectral balance: a harsh voice and a neutral voice at the same level read within a few dB. The existing Leveler therefore cannot tell a harsh voice from a neutral one of the same level beyond those few dB.
- Sharpness is normalised by total loudness, so it describes balance, not level, which is attractive for low-level behaviour; but it is a scalar fed by specific loudness (the costly part), weights only above 16 Bark, and the variants differ. It covers harsh highs but has no counterpart for boom; no standard "boominess" metric was found. A cheap proxy (band-energy ratios across frequency, or a spectral centroid) captures most of the same idea at trivial cost.
- Gullfoss's "Boost" parameter shows the level-dependent balance idea in a product (it emphasizes bass and reduces mids to mimic a volume change), but Gullfoss boosts, so it cannot be used as-is. A cut-only level-dependent tilt would need the acoustic level, which a plug-in before the volume slider does not see (brief; cross-reference to the building researcher).

- Seefeldt's paper text was not accessible; the AES 2012 real-time paper was not read.
- No product or paper found that uses sharpness as a control variable; no boominess metric found.

- The Bark-to-Hz mapping (16 Bark is about 3 kHz) is general knowledge, not verified in a fetched source.

## Key Question 4: Hearing-aid and hearing-protection approaches: multichannel WDRC, per-channel MPO from discomfort levels, comfort programs, level-dependent protectors

### Takeaway
Hearing-aid compression supplies concrete parameter ranges (3-16 channels, ratios under 3-4, release 50 ms to 2 s) and a warning that many fast channels smear spectral contrast; MPO, EN 352 and acoustic-shock limiters act near discomfort or exposure limits (82-118 dB), so they would not act at ordinary low listening levels unless thresholds are fitted low per person, as in the hyperacusis limiter of Eddins et al.

### Cited Findings
- Patel and Panahi (a hearing-aid compression study using nine bands; publication year not confirmed in the pages read): modern hearing aids use "3 to 16" channels (they propose nine unequal bands centred at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 kHz); compression ratio "is generally kept below 4", "high compression ratios (> 3) are avoided" for fast-acting systems, tests used R = 2; threshold "typically kept around 65 dB SPL" with knee width 20 dB; fast release "50 to 100ms", slow "800ms to 2s"; attack "5 to 30 ms" in the introduction but "5 to 20ms" in the parameter list [CONFLICT], tests used 10 ms; fast systems introduce "pumping and breathing effects" in noise; in slow systems "gain drops to a low value right after an intense sound" — [Patel and Panahi, PMC7380331](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7380331)
- [SNIPPET] Fast-versus-slow compression evidence conflicts: Gatehouse and colleagues (2006) found slow release better for listening comfort and fast release better for intelligibility; a 2008 study (30 people with mild-to-moderate loss; fast 9 ms attack and 90 ms release, slow 900 ms and 1,500 ms) found both settings beat linear processing for reverberant speech in quiet, with no difference between fast and slow; Phonak material says no established method identifies who benefits from slow compression — [ISAAR 2011-52](https://proceedings.isaar.eu/index.php/isaarproc/article/download/2011-52/168); [ASHA 2008 study](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/1092-4388(2008/07-0196)); [Hearing Review: personalized compression](https://hearingreview.com/hearing-products/hearing-aids/speech-in-noise/empowering-patients-with-personalized-compression)
- [SNIPPET] A search summary reports that a 2016 Moore and Sek study (Trends in Hearing) found listeners on average favoured slow compression, especially for music, with pronounced individual differences, and that Moore's 2008 paper (Trends in Amplification 12:103-112; only an abstract was seen) suggests testing sensitivity to temporal fine structure to choose compression speed; the summary gives no URL for the 2016 study (PMC5017572 was among the hits but is not confirmed as that paper), while a 2015 Moore and Sek proceedings paper with 18 hearing-impaired participants found only partial support for the temporal-fine-structure idea — [ISAAR 2015 paper](https://proceedings.isaar.eu/index.php/isaarproc/article/view/2015-26); [PMC5017572, unconfirmed match](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5017572)
- [SNIPPET] Spectral smearing: Plomp (1988) predicted heavy compression across many channels would flatten speech spectra; fast compression in narrow channels shrinks peak-to-valley contrast; no consensus on ideal channel count; "anti-smearing filters" exist in newer devices — [PMC2605015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2605015); [AudiologyOnline: compression effects](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/hearing-aid-compression-effects-speed-770)
- Per-channel MPO and LDL: frequency-specific loudness discomfort levels have "long been a component of best practice"; LDL is "very difficult to predict" from the audiogram; aided output should not exceed LDL nor sit far below it ("a Goldilocks thing"); Bentler and Cooley (2001), "over 500 ears": for 40-60 dB losses the LDL range was about 50 dB and "only 32% were within 5 dB of the average"; coupler tests of six premium brands showed measured output well below NAL-NL2's predicted MPO for all but one (two about 20 dB lower), and "it might be the WDRC, not the AGCo, that controls the maximum output" — [Canadian Audiologist](https://canadianaudiologist.ca/15105-2/)
- [SNIPPET] NAL-NL2 uses a loudness model to maximize intelligibility while keeping loudness at or below normal, caps compression ratio, returns a compression threshold, ratio, gain and MPO per channel, and prescribes MPO from thresholds, not measured UCL — [AudiologyOnline Q&A](https://www.audiologyonline.com/ask-the-experts/does-resound-aventa-prescribe-mpo-17842); [Keidser et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4627149)
- Eddins and colleagues (2024) device for loudness hyperacusis: output limiting holds back loud sounds while "unity gain passes quieter, tolerable ones"; "infinite:1 compression in each channel" in an "eight-channel limiting scheme"; the limit starts at 35 dB SPL and is raised in 1-2 dB steps until running speech (presented at 50-80 dB SPL) is judged "loud, but OK"; the main detector uses "32 ms attack time constant, 256 ms release time constant" plus a transient detector with "< 1 ms" attack and release; a separate 16-band equalizer handles gain shaping; LDLs are displayed for comparison but the limit comes from the patient's judgement; no outcome numbers in this paper (a companion field trial was not read) — [Eddins et al., PMC11192565](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11192565)
- Assistive-audio study (Popescu, Frost, Cernak, 2025, evaluated offline on synthetic mixtures, no real-time implementation): for distressing "trigger" sounds (not voices) a dynamic range compressor (threshold -35 dB, ratio 30:1, attack 0.01 ms, release 100 ms) scored best in a listening test with 133 neurodivergent and 47 control listeners (trigger rating, lower is better, 38.22 versus 66.71 unprocessed for the neurodivergent group; processed outputs were added to a simulated noise-cancelling path), while the EQ barely helped (64.67; it was five shelving filters tuned to maximize signal-to-noise ratio on synthetic mixtures, including a +1.6 dB boost at 5 kHz, so it was not designed for listener comfort) — [arXiv 2509.10202](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10202v1)
- Level-dependent hearing protectors: EN 352-4 criterion is "85dB(A) at the ear" (tested with miniature microphones in human ear canals; the page lists no attack or release requirements) — [SATRA EN 352-4](https://satra.com/ppe/EN352-4.php); EN 352-8:2008 (entertainment audio ear-muffs) requires "The mean value of 16 measurements plus one standard deviation shall not exceed 82dB(A)" — [SATRA EN 352-8](https://www.satra.com/ppe/EN352-8.php)
- [UNCERTAIN, SNIPPET] A search summary says the 2020 edition of EN 352-4 replaced the 2001 text that the SATRA page describes — [iTeh: SIST EN 352-4:2021](https://iteh.es/catalog/standards/sist/bfddce39-c1b5-4c03-a70c-6066aafe57fe/sist-en-352-4-2021)
- Apple Hearing Protection (AirPods Pro 2 and 3) reduces sound "around you": "In Transparency and Adaptive modes, attenuation increases as your environment gets louder"; Noise Cancellation gives "a consistent amount of hearing protection"; it is not rated above 110 dBA sustained; media playback is not mentioned — [Apple Support 120850](https://support.apple.com/en-gw/120850)
- [CONFLICT, SNIPPET] A search summary of Apple's regional pages says the feature is on in all three listening modes, but that Apple's Australian page ties Loud Sound Reduction to Transparency mode, unlike the UK and Indian pages; the summary also says no fixed decibel rating was given — [Apple AU hearing health](https://www.apple.com/au/airpods-pro/hearing-health/); [Apple UK hearing health](https://www.apple.com/uk/airpods-pro/hearing-health/)
- Apple's headphone-level feature lets you pick a limit "75 to 100 decibels in increments of 5" and "automatically reduce the loudness" above it (July 2020 iOS 14 article; newer guides call it Reduce Loud Audio, MacRumors says Reduce Loud Sounds [CONFLICT on naming]; Apple's own page returned only navigation text, so official wording is missing) — [MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/how-to/reduce-loud-sounds-when-listening-over-headphones/)
- Call-centre acoustic shock: UK HSE says headset limiters since 1991 stop noise "above 118 dB" and are defined by level only (the only frequency mention is "high frequency" in the incident definition) — [HSE: acoustic shock](https://hse.gov.uk/NOISE/acoustic.htm); Jabra SafeTone: PeakStop clamps spikes at 118 dB (RMS), SafeTone 2.0 PeakStop at 105 dB (RMS), IntelliTone adjusts average level "down to 85 dB" over a working day, industry guideline G616 recommends an acoustic-shock device limit of "102 dB SPL RMS measured at DRP for all frequencies", "intelligent" protection analyses harmful sounds and removes or reduces them "without affecting the ongoing conversation", and BalancedVoice processes incoming calls "to be crisper and clearer without increasing the sound level" (72% preference in a vendor-cited Fraunhofer test; algorithm not described) — [Jabra hearing protection white paper](https://jabra.cn/_/media/Files/Hearing-protection/Jabra-Hearing-Protection-White-Paper_270522.pdf)
- [SNIPPET] ITU-T P.360 (2006) caps the sound pressure handset and headset earpieces can produce; numeric limits were not retrieved — [ITU-T P.360](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-P.360-200607-I/en)

### Inferences
- The Eddins device is architecturally the same family as Ariel's system (cut-only limiting, unity gain below threshold), but multichannel with per-person thresholds. Because its limit starts at 35 dB SPL and is raised until speech is "loud, but OK", it can act at ordinary conversation levels; a typical MPO set near discomfort would not (cross-reference: per-person tuning researcher for how to find the thresholds).
- Absolute per-band thresholds are calibrated to SPL at the eardrum. A plug-in before the Windows volume slider sees only digital level, so the slider changes the acoustic level without the limiter knowing; absolute dBFS thresholds therefore cannot represent "low listening level" unless the slider position is read (brief; cross-reference: building researcher).
- Transferable ideas: few channels with low ratios and slow-ish release (hearing-aid ranges), a slow detector plus a fast transient detector (Eddins 32/256 ms plus under 1 ms), and thresholds fitted from the person's own judgement. Evidence favours slow release for comfort (Gatehouse, Moore and Sek average preference), which supports a gentle, slow design over a fast one.
- Hearing-protection standards and acoustic-shock limiters bound exposure (82-118 dB) and are level-only; they do not address timbre and would never engage at low volume.
- The assistive-audio result shows level-based compression beating static EQ for distressing sounds, but those are loud transients, not harsh voices; do not generalise to this complaint [UNCERTAIN].

## Key Question 6: Starting parameters for speech: band edges, thresholds, ratios and limits, attack and release, maximum cut per band

### Takeaway
Sourced starting values exist for sibilance bands, de-ess timing and hearing-aid compression, but none is validated for a cut-only playback system aimed at sustained harshness or boom; harshness (2-5 kHz) and mud (150-500 Hz) bands rest on loose sources and maximum cuts are not sourced. A proposed set is given as an inference and must be validated by listening.

### Cited Findings
#### Band edges
- Sibilance: female 5-8 kHz, male 3-6 kHz — [Apple Support: Logic Pro](https://support.apple.com/en-ae/102006); [CONFLICT] Wikipedia gives female 6-8 kHz [SNIPPET] — [Wikipedia: De-essing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-essing); Sound On Sound says sibilance is "usually focused somewhere in a region from 4-10kHz" — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/techniques-vocal-de-essing); FabFilter says typical "s" sounds are "around 8 to 10 kHz" and its trigger range spans 2-20 kHz — [Pro-DS basic controls](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/basiccontrols)
- Harshness: [SNIPPET, CONFLICT] blogs place harsh energy at roughly 2-5 kHz, with one guide grouping harshness with sibilance at 5-8 kHz and a metal guide at 5-10 kHz; sibilance 5-9 kHz; mud 200-500 Hz or 150-500 Hz; pop-vocal high-pass near 100 Hz for male and 150 Hz for female; rumble filtering at 80-100 Hz. These are loose producer guides, and the search summary does not say which page gave which figure — [Vocalfy](https://vocalfy.com/blog/how-to-eq-vocals/); [Music Guy Mixing](https://www.musicguymixing.com/?p=5838); [Emastered: female vocal EQ](https://emastered.com/blog/female-vocal-eq)

- Hearing-aid channel centres: 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 kHz — [Patel and Panahi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7380331)

#### Thresholds: absolute versus relative
- Relative or adaptive: Pro-Q automatic threshold (adjusts to the level of the band-filtered detection signal, so it is band-referenced, not across-frequency) — [Pro-Q 4](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq); soothe3 Soft mode with an "adaptive threshold" — [soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3); iZotope RX De-ess Relative (default) — [iZotope RX 9](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-ess/index.html); Logic DeEsser 2 Relative "for lower-level signals" — [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-ae/102006); Pro-DS Single Vocal with Threshold at -INF dB, where all sibilance is reduced by about the same amount — [Pro-DS basic controls](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/basiccontrols)
- Absolute or level-dependent: Logic Absolute "for higher-level signals" — [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-ae/102006); iZotope Absolute in dBFS — [iZotope RX 9](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-ess/index.html); soothe Hard mode is "level-dependent" — [soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3); soothe live needs depth reset per source — [soothe live manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe-live); hearing aids use a threshold of about 65 dB SPL with a 20 dB knee — [Patel and Panahi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7380331)
- soothe live flags resonant frequencies and then checks each "against the surrounding spectrum" (relative across frequency), but it also factors in resonance level and is "more level-dependent than soothe2", so relative detection across frequency does not make it level-independent — [soothe live manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe-live)

#### Ratios and limits
- Hearing aids: ratio generally below 4, below 3 for fast-acting systems, R = 2 in the tests — [Patel and Panahi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7380331); Eddins limiter: infinite:1 in each of eight channels — [Eddins et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11192565); Pro-Q dynamic range -30 to +30 dB, with no ratio, knee, attack or release values given on the page read — [Pro-Q 4](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq)

#### Attack, release, lookahead, detector windows
- De-ess: sub-1 ms attack and release about 10 ms — [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/techniques-vocal-de-essing); 5-10 ms attack and 40-60 ms release [SNIPPET; the search summary names "one Unison blog post" without saying which of its two pages] — [Unison: de-essing](https://unison.audio/de-essing/); [Unison: what is a de-esser](https://unison.audio/what-is-a-de-esser/); lookahead up to 15 ms, about 10 ms for vocals — [Pro-DS advanced controls](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-ds/using/advancedcontrols)
- Hearing aids: attack 5-30 ms (or 5-20 ms, tests 10 ms), fast release 50-100 ms, slow 800 ms-2 s — [Patel and Panahi](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7380331); Eddins detector 32 ms attack and 256 ms release plus a transient detector under 1 ms — [Eddins et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11192565)
- soothe: fast release gives audible phase distortion on drastic cuts and a breathy sound, slow release is steadier; attack and release are frequency-dependent — [soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3)
- Loudness: BS.1770 gating blocks of 400 ms with 75% overlap — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)

- Rationale: attack and release sit between de-ess values (5-10 ms, 40-60 ms) and hearing-aid values (release 50 ms-2 s, Eddins 32/256 ms); the boom band is slower because low-frequency periods are long (a 150 Hz cycle is about 6.7 ms) and, by general signal-processing reasoning that is not sourced here, fast gain changes within a cycle would distort the waveform; ratios follow the hearing-aid guidance (under 3, tests at 2); max cut starts small because manuals warn that deep cuts mangle sound and cause phase distortion, and soothe live's "roughly up to 9 dB" suggests the moderate regime.

Do not reference a band to its own long-term average, which would absorb persistent harshness.

## Key Question 7: Which 2 or 3 methods best fit a system that may only turn sound down, and does a simple or static approach beat a dynamic one?

### Takeaway
Best fit, in order: (1) a cut-only dynamic EQ with 2-3 frequency bands and relative (across-frequency) detection, slow-ish release and small maximum cut; (2) a gentle static comfort EQ as an always-on baseline; (3) a soothe-style spectral or resonance suppressor as the automatic, heavier version of (1) if fixed bands prove too speaker-dependent. A static approach alone wins only if the harshness is a stable property of the headphone or listener; for voice-specific harshness a static curve must be set for a compromise.

### Cited Findings
- Every row of the table below draws on the findings cited in Key Questions 1 to 6. The findings that most affect the ranking are repeated here with their sources.
- Zero-latency, frequency-selective dynamic processing ships in current products: Pro-MB Dynamic Phase has "zero latency operation" and "no pre-ringing effects" — [Pro-MB processing mode](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-mb/using/processingmode); soothe3 Low latency is 0 samples at 48 kHz — [soothe3 manual](https://oeksound.com/manuals/soothe3); Pro-Q 4 offers a Zero Latency mode — [Pro-Q 4: Dynamic EQ](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/dynamic-eq)

Ranking, judged honestly:
The ranking is by fit to the stated complaint (voice-specific harshness and boom). The build order is the reverse: start with the static layer (rank 2), then add the dynamic layer (rank 1) only if a static cut deep enough to help dulls everything else.
1. Cut-only dynamic EQ (2-3 bands, relative detection). It is a documented, shipped method that is frequency-selective, can be set to only cut (a dynamic band with 0 dB static gain and a negative range, as in Pro-Q; the help page equates negative range with compression but does not say cut-only outright), should work at any listening level when detection is relative across frequency (my reasoning: a ratio between band levels is unchanged when the whole signal is scaled; the one documented across-frequency check, soothe live's, also factors in level), and has zero-latency implementations. Risks: pumping, per-voice tuning, and the lack of speech-specific numbers.
2. Gentle static comfort EQ. It is the simplest, artifact-free, level-independent baseline, and the right first layer if the cause is the headphone or the listener. Its cost is that it dulls every sound.
3. Spectral or resonance suppression (soothe-style). It handles speaker variety automatically, but it is the most complex option, its standard mode has tens of milliseconds of latency, and its manuals give no speech guidance.
- Why the others rank lower: wideband gain cannot change spectral balance (the existing Leveler's limitation); multiband absolute-threshold limiters and hearing-aid MPO are calibrated to levels the plug-in cannot see and do not act at low listening levels; Gullfoss boosts; the ISO 532 specific-loudness limiter is unproven in real time; consumer features do not target the complaint.

Static versus dynamic:
- A static approach beats a dynamic one on simplicity, artifacts, latency and low-level behaviour, and it should be the first layer. For the stated complaint, which is voice-specific ("sometimes a woman with a high voice, or a man with a deep voice"), one static curve is wrong for the other voices, so a shallow dynamic layer on top adds the selectivity the static layer lacks.
- The dynamic layer should be restrained: few bands, slow release, small maximum cut, so it behaves like a static EQ that moves only when a band is out of balance. If listening tests show a mild static cut (a few dB) fixes most voices without audible dulling, static alone wins. The evidence is indirect; no source compares them on this complaint.
- What would change the ranking: a headphone-specific resonance (static wins), mostly sibilant harshness (split-band de-esser first), boom only on certain voices (low dynamic band first).
