# Why some voices sound "too hard" on headphones even at low overall playback level: causes

Reading guide. Research date 2026-11-16. Scope is causes only: (a) the ear and perception, (b) the voice and the recording and processing chain, (c) the headphones and output hardware, (d) the listener's own sensitivity. Protection methods, in-plug-in implementation and per-person tuning belong to other researchers; one-line pointers are written "->". Tags: **(computed)** = my own arithmetic on the cited inputs, run in memory with nothing saved; **(secondary)** = taken from a search-result summary whose page I did not open in full, so verify before relying; untagged = from a page or document I fetched. PDFs and the BS.1770 document (Suzuki 2024, Sottek 2023, Fenton & Lee 2017, ITU-R BS.1770-5, the arXiv 2103.08203 paper and the two Tinnitus UK PDFs) were extracted and read as raw text; ordinary web pages were read through the fetch tool's text extraction, so their quotes are as that extraction returned them. Hearing-health content is informational only and is not a diagnosis. Context from the assignment: wired headphones on the onboard Realtek output; own plug-in with a K-weighted Leveler and a Safety Cap placed before the Windows volume slider; the complaint is that some high female voices and some deep male voices (mostly Zoom and YouTube) sound "too hard" even at low volume.

## 1. Which bands make speech sound harsh/shrill versus boomy/heavy, what are typical F0 and formants for high female and deep male voices, and where do their harmonics land relative to the ear's most sensitive region?

### Takeaway
Harsh, piercing speech sits in roughly 1.5-5 kHz (presence, "harsh") and 3-8 kHz (sibilance, higher for women), which is where the outer ear peaks (about 2.7 kHz) and where listeners rate sounds most unpleasant (about 2-5 kHz); boomy, heavy speech sits in roughly 100-400 Hz (mud to 500 Hz) and is inflated by close-mic proximity effect. High female voices (F0 about 220-250 Hz, F3 about 2.9 kHz, F4 about 4.2 kHz) put formant energy and a sparse harmonic grid on the ear's most sensitive band; deep male voices (F0 about 100-130 Hz, F3 about 2.5 kHz) put their energy in 100-400 Hz with a dense harmonic grid above it.

### Cited Findings

**Harsh versus boomy bands (practitioner guidance, not psychoacoustic measurements)**
- A vocal-EQ guide lists: boomy 100-400 Hz; muddy 100-400 Hz (its FAQ says 200-500 Hz); boxy 400-800 Hz; nasal 800 Hz-1.5 kHz; harsh 1.5-5 kHz (elsewhere "2 kHz and 5 kHz"); presence 1.5-5 kHz (FAQ: 3-5 kHz); sibilance 5-8 kHz ("S" and "T"); air 12-20 kHz; it says hearing is keenest near 2-5 kHz. The guide's own ranges are not fully consistent and it gives no separate male/female ranges. — [iZotope, How to EQ vocals](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/how-to-eq-vocals)
- Most "ess" energy falls at 3-6 kHz, "where our hearing is relatively sensitive"; a cheap-mic example has main energy near 4-6 kHz plus content above 10 kHz; many vocal mics carry "a broad boost somewhere around 5kHz" which also raises sibilance; "Female voices tend to have higher-frequency esses than male ones" (no separate numbers given); brighter mics, high-shelf boosts, firm top-end boosts on the master output and compression can "magnify the sibilant frequencies"; cheap, distortion-prone gear makes loud esses worse. — [Sound On Sound, Managing sibilance](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/managing-sibilance)
- (secondary) Other vocal-EQ cheat sheets disagree on band names: nasal/honky 500 Hz-1 kHz (mastering.com), 0.8-1.2 kHz (Nail the Mix, RySup Audio), 1-2 kHz (Music Guy Mixing), a nasal-harshness problem at 2.5-3.5 kHz (Orphiq); harsh 2-5 kHz (mastering.com); boomy 200-300 Hz tied to proximity effect (Orphiq). — [mastering.com cheat sheet](https://mastering.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/EQ-CHEAT-SHEET.pdf), [Orphiq](https://orphiq.com/resources/vocal-eq-cheat-sheet)
- Proximity effect (pressure-gradient mics such as cardioids) acts "within about two feet" of the source. Ideal cardioid, free field, values at 100 Hz: "about 21 dB at one inch", "6 dB at six inches", "3 dB at twelve inches", "under 1 dB at twenty-four inches"; real mics read lower because most include bass roll-off; the text also gives a ceiling "up to 16 dB" that the page does not reconcile with its own 21 dB figure; the boost rises at 6 dB per octave below a corner frequency that moves from roughly 45 Hz at two feet to over 1 kHz at one inch; omnidirectional mics show essentially none; "boomy" radio voices are the page's everyday example. — [cmtext, Studio gear ch. 2](https://cmtext.com/studio/chapter2_mics2.php)
- (secondary) Other brands' curves: about 10 dB near 125 Hz at roughly 2 inches (Sennheiser e609, Mix Online column); about 12 dB at 50 Hz and below from just under four inches (DPA, Tangible Technology write-up). Shure's own pages describe the effect only qualitatively. — [Mix Online](https://www.mixonline.com/?p=98172), [Tangible Technology](https://www.tangible-technology.com/microphones/proximity/proximity.htm)
- (secondary) Raised vocal effort moves voice quality from breathy toward tense or pressed and adds harmonic energy (a PeerJ article citing Traunmueller & Eriksson 2000); the spectral-tilt specifics were not confirmed. — [PeerJ 14944](https://peerj.com/articles/14944)

**The ear's most sensitive region**
- The ear canal acts as a resonator "enhancing sounds in the range 2-5 kiloHertz"; the page gives no dB value for the canal. — [HyperPhysics, Ear and hearing](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/sound/ear.html)
- Unaided ear canal plus concha: "The peak occurs at about 2700 Hz", with "surrounding resonances between 1500 and 4000Hz", and a gain "on the order of about 15-20 dB". — [Venema, AudiologyOnline 2008](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/common-clinical-encounters-59-but-909)
- (secondary) Goode (MEMRO 2006 abstract): adult gain about 15-25 dB peaking near 2.7 kHz, a second bump of about 10 dB near 5 kHz tentatively attributed to the concha, and individual variation above 2 kHz of up to about 20 dB (larger concha/pinna/canal openings tend to go with higher gain). — [Goode](https://www.orl.uzh.ch/memro/program/3.9_Goode.pdf)
- (secondary) Shaw (1974) puts the main external-ear resonance near 2.6 kHz, with the concha sustaining the response over almost an octave; a 2003 mailing-list post by Allen quotes Shaw for about 12 dB at 2.5 kHz (pinna, canal and drum together) and argues that the classical canal-resonance picture is "a false concept" (contested; the figure also varies with what is counted: canal alone, canal plus concha, or whole head). — [Shaw, SCOPE 24](https://scope.dge.carnegiescience.edu/SCOPE_24/SCOPE_24_1.5_Shaw.pdf), [Allen post](https://auditory.org/postings/2003/585.html)
- Sounds in "around 2,000 to 5,000 Hz" were judged unpleasant; the amygdala modulates auditory-cortex activity, and activity in both regions "varied in direct relation to the ratings of perceived unpleasantness". The release never addresses loudness. — [Newcastle University press release on Kumar et al. 2012](https://www.ncl.ac.uk/press/articles/archive/2012/10/nastynoiseswhydowerecoilatunpleasantsounds.html); (secondary: search listing only, full text not read) study record: [Kumar et al., J Neurosci 32(41), 2012](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Features-versus-Feelings-Dissociable-Representations-of/9984303904102771)
- (computed, ISO 226:2003 formula with the IoSR parameter arrays) the threshold of hearing is lowest near 3.15-4 kHz (-6.0 and -5.4 dB SPL versus +2.4 dB at 1 kHz and +26.5 dB at 100 Hz), and the 40-phon contour needs 35.6 dB SPL at 3.15 kHz versus 40.0 dB at 1 kHz and 64.4 dB at 100 Hz. Full tables are in question 2. — [IoSR toolbox iso226.m](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IoSR-Surrey/MatlabToolbox/master/%2Biosr/%2Bauditory/iso226.m)

**Typical voices**
- Adult male F0 "from 90 to 155 Hz", adult female "from 165 to 255 Hz". — [Wikipedia, Voice frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_frequency)
- (computed from the public Hillenbrand et al. 1995 data: 45 men and 48 women, 12 American-English vowels each, steady-state values in "hVd" words; 540 and 576 tokens) **Men:** F0 mean 131 Hz (token SD 22, range 90-224); per-talker mean F0 min 96, 10th percentile 106, median 131, 90th percentile 154, max 192; formants F1 524, F2 1508, F3 2517, F4 3541 Hz. **Women:** F0 mean 220 Hz (SD 23, range 149-307); per-talker mean F0 min 162, 10th percentile 195, median 220, 90th percentile 248, max 259; formants F1 617, F2 1763, F3 2866, F4 4166 Hz. In the vowel /iy/ ("heed") men average F2 2323 and F3 3001 Hz, women F2 2761 and F3 3373 Hz. — [Hillenbrand et al. 1995 data (GitHub)](https://github.com/santiagobarreda/hillenbrand_et_al_1995); original paper: JASA 97, 3099-3111 (1995), per [the data page's search listing](https://github.com/santiagobarreda/hillenbrand_et_al_1995)

**Where harmonics land (computed)**
- Auditory-filter width: ERB(f) = 24.7 x (4.37 x f[kHz] + 1) Hz (Glasberg & Moore 1990), about 349 Hz at 3 kHz. — [Wikipedia, ERB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalent_rectangular_bandwidth)
- Critical-band rate (my use of the standard Zwicker-Terhardt Bark approximation, formula from my own knowledge, not retrieved): 1 kHz = 8.5 Bark; 2.7 kHz = 15.0; 3 kHz = 15.6; 4 kHz = 17.3; 6 kHz = 19.6; 8 kHz = 21.3; 10 kHz = 22.4.

### Inferences
- A high female voice stacks three things on the ear's most sensitive band: F3 (about 2.9 kHz) and F4 (about 4.2 kHz) energy, a handful of widely spaced harmonics (#10-#14 at roughly 2.2-3.6 kHz for F0 220-260 Hz), and sibilants that sit higher than men's. In the vowel /iy/ even F2 and F3 (2.76 and 3.37 kHz on average) fall inside 2.5-4 kHz.
- A deep male voice puts its fundamental and first harmonics in the 100-400 Hz "boomy/muddy" zone, where proximity effect also acts (+6 dB at 6 inches and +21 dB at 1 inch at 100 Hz in the ideal cardioid); its F3 (about 2.5 kHz) sits at the lower edge of the outer-ear region, so a deep voice with a presence boost can still sound harsh.
- Hypothesis, no direct source: with only about 1.2-1.6 harmonics per auditory filter at 3 kHz, a woman's harmonics are not averaged much, so one that lands on the 2.7 kHz canal peak (for example #12 at 2640 Hz for F0 220 Hz) can stand out as a tonal "ping"; with about 2.3-3.5 harmonics per filter, a man's harmonics produce envelope fluctuation at F0 (90-155 Hz), which lies inside the 15-300 Hz roughness range (question 3), so high-frequency harshness in deep voices may read as buzz or roughness rather than shrillness.
- At low playback level an excess at 2-5 kHz is more salient than an excess at 100-400 Hz (question 2), so "too hard even when low" points more toward 1.5-8 kHz than toward bass, unless an unusually large low-frequency boost is present (headphone, seal, proximity effect).

### Gaps
- Long-term average speech spectra by sex and band (Byrne et al. 1994 and Hopkins et al.) were not retrievable (scispace PDF empty; IoA PDF returned 404), so I cannot give a sourced dB difference between male and female speech at 2.5-4 kHz or at 125 Hz.
- The Hillenbrand values are steady-state vowels read in "hVd" words by American-English talkers (one dialect region); conversational, emotional, effortful or other-language speech, and the effect of vocal effort on spectral tilt, are not covered (Traunmueller & Eriksson 2000 was only seen secondhand). F3 and F4 means exclude tokens the dataset marks as unmeasurable (zero entries): F3 is present for 532 of 540 male and 560 of 576 female tokens and F4 for 446 and 508 (computed), so the F4 means describe a subset of tokens.
- No measured fricative spectra by sex; "female esses are higher" has no numbers beyond the generic 3-6 kHz and 5-8 kHz guidance.
- The EQ-guide band names are practitioner conventions that disagree between authors; treat them as indicative only. They come from music-mixing guides written for sung and recorded vocals (the iZotope worked example is a male lead vocal in D major), so applying them to conversational speech heard through headphones is my assumption.
- Outer-ear gain is quoted as 12, 15-20 or 15-25 dB depending on scope; Shaw's 1974 paper itself was not retrieved; Kumar et al. 2012 full text was not read (the press release gives 2-5 kHz, and the "2.5-5.5 kHz" figure I had expected was not verified).
- The per-ERB harmonic arithmetic and the resolvability rule of thumb are my own; no source on how many speech harmonics are actually resolved at low level was retrieved.

## 2. What does ISO 226:2023 say per band at low levels, how well does the Leveler's K-weighting (BS.1770; about +4 dB high shelf above about 1.7 kHz, about 38 Hz high-pass) already account for treble and bass, and what does it miss?

### Takeaway
ISO 226:2023 (within 0.6 dB of the 2003 edition) shows that the ear's bass sensitivity collapses at low level (a 100 Hz tone needs 48.4 dB SPL for 20 phon, 28 dB more than 1 kHz) while the 3-4 kHz advantage persists (+4 to +6 dB versus 1 kHz at 20-40 phon). The fixed K-weighting therefore over-credits bass at low level (about +10 to +27 dB at 100 Hz), is within about 3 dB of pure-tone sensitivity at 2-4 kHz (and about 1 dB for octave-band noise), and cannot see "harshness" at all because it is a loudness-level meter.

### Cited Findings

**ISO 226**
- ISO 226:2023 is the revision that followed ISO 226:2003 (which had itself fully revised the earlier standard after significant errors were reported in 1985); differences from 2003 are at most 0.6 dB (range -0.6 to +0.3 dB; over 0.4 dB only at 10 phon; within 0.3 dB above 10 phon), and the 2023 standard "can be regarded as the same as the 2003 edition in terms of practical use". The contours are for pure tones from the front in a free sound field, and the experimental equal-loudness values behind them typically have a standard deviation of about 5-6 dB. — [Suzuki, Takeshima & Kurakata 2024, Acoust. Sci. & Tech. 45(1)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast/45/1/45_e23.66/_pdf)
- The updated introduction "emphasizes that the equal-loudness-level contours are only applicable to pure tones, since there are insufficient data to estimate their validity for other sound types such as broadband noise and noise with prominent tones". — [Sottek et al. 2023, Forum Acusticum](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000585.pdf)

**K-weighting (ITU-R BS.1770-5)**
- The algorithm is: "K" frequency weighting; mean-square per channel; channel-weighted summation; gating of 400 ms blocks overlapping by 75% with thresholds at -70 LKFS and at -10 dB relative to the level after the first threshold. Stage 1 "accounts for the acoustic effects of the head, where the head is modelled as a rigid sphere"; stage 2 is "a simple highpass filter" (the RLB weighting). — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)
- The Recommendation says the algorithm is effective "for audio programmes that are typical of broadcast content" but "is not, in general, suitable for use to estimate the subjective loudness of pure tones", and that measured loudness "is an estimation of subjective loudness and involves some degree of uncertainty depending on listeners, audio material and listening conditions". — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)
- Validation: 336 broadcast sequences (music, drama, sport, news, effects, advertisements, speech in several languages), overall correlation r = 0.977 (the mono Leq(RLB) predecessor r = 0.982); subjects matched the loudness of the whole signal, "not just the speech component"; the reference item was English female speech. — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)
- (computed from the Recommendation's 48 kHz coefficient tables) K-weighting relative to its 1 kHz value: 38 Hz -6.7 dB; 50 Hz -4.6; 63 Hz -3.4; 100 Hz -1.8; 200 Hz -1.0; 500 Hz -0.7; 1.5 kHz +1.3; 1.7 kHz +1.8; 2 kHz +2.4; 3 kHz +3.1; 4 kHz and above +3.3 (flat to 16 kHz; absolute high-frequency gain about +4 dB). The stage-2 high-pass is a double pole near 38 Hz (about -6 dB there), so its -3 dB point is near 60 Hz. — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)

**K-weighting against listeners on band-limited noise**
- Octave-band pink noise (63 Hz to 8 kHz, reference level 75 dB(Z); 13 listeners matched each band to a 1 kHz octave-band reference over a Genelec 8040A loudspeaker 2 m away, so this is loudspeaker, not headphone, listening; 80 comparisons per listener, 1040 responses). K-weighted meter error versus listeners, in dB: 63 Hz -7.24; 125 Hz -3.79; 250 Hz -1.11; 500 Hz +3.05; 1 kHz 0; 2 kHz +1.09; 4 kHz +1.18; 8 kHz -1.04; RMS error 3.18 dB. Worked example: at 63 Hz listeners applied about 9.975 dB of gain to match the 1 kHz band, but the meter implied only 2.73 dB, "an overestimation of energy made by the algorithm" (so a negative error means the meter reads the band louder than listeners judged it; I infer positive values mean the meter reads it quieter). The paper notes a 1 dB dip in the K-weighted response at the 500 Hz band that it attributes possibly to window size, and observed disagreements for vocal stems and narrow-band sources. The authors conclude that "a greater roll off and/or change of cutoff is required in the filter to match the perceived response". The paper's own notice says its abstract and precis were peer reviewed but the complete manuscript was not. — [Fenton & Lee, AES 143rd Convention 2017](https://eprints.hud.ac.uk/33794/1/AES%20143_AlternativeFilters_FentonLee4_Final.pdf)
- Modern standardized loudness models also deviate from the pure-tone contours: RMS difference to ISO 226:2023 is 5.55 dB (ISO 532-1), 3.04 dB (ISO 532-3) and 1.57 dB (ECMA 418-2); between 1 and 5 kHz ECMA 418-2 matches best while the others "overestimate the loudness of tonal components in this range"; for noise signals, particularly sub-critical bandwidth, the authors recommend ECMA 418-2. BS.1770 was not part of that comparison. The same paper says the Zwicker method behind ISO 532-1 aims at the 1987 contours while the other two aim at the 2003 data (my inference: part of ISO 532-1's larger difference reflects that older target). — [Sottek et al. 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000585.pdf)

### Inferences
- Bass: the fixed K-weighting over-counts bass relative to a pure-tone ear, and by more at lower level (100 Hz: +26.5 dB at 20 phon, +10.6 dB at 80 phon). A K-weighted Leveler or Cap therefore errs toward turning bass-heavy voices down; a deep voice that is "too hard at low level" is not explained by the Leveler under-measuring bass. Even the noise-band data show over-credit at 63-125 Hz (-7.2 and -3.8 dB).
- Treble loudness is approximately right: within about +/-3 dB of pure-tone sensitivity at 2-4 kHz and within about 1.2 dB for octave-band noise at 75 dB(Z). The 20-phon case is the worst, with K crediting 3.15 kHz 2.6 dB less than the ear does, so low playback level makes the 2.5-4 kHz region slightly more important than K assumes. A harsh high voice is therefore not being mis-measured as much quieter than it is.
- Above about 6 kHz the pure-tone contours say K over-credits by 8-18 dB, but the only noise-band measurement (8 kHz octave, 75 dB(Z)) shows about 1 dB; treat the pure-tone number as an upper bound, because ISO 226 does not apply to noise-like sibilants. Either way the loudness measure is not under-counting sibilant energy.
- Net: the weakness of a K-weighted Leveler/Cap against harsh voices is not mis-weighting but that loudness is not harshness. Two voices leveled to the same LKFS can differ strongly in spectral balance, sharpness and tonal prominence (question 3). -> protection-methods researcher: whether a spectral-shape-aware stage is needed.
- Leveling by K-weighted loudness at low playback level tends to leave treble-rich voices perceptually louder than bass-rich voices (K credits bass 10-35 dB too much and 2.5-3.2 kHz about 0-3 dB too little at 20-40 phon). This is a qualitative consequence of the table, not a measured speech result.

### Gaps
- ISO 226 is pure-tone only. I found no equal-loudness or K-weighting accuracy data for voiced speech or other harmonic complexes at low playback level (below roughly 50 dB SPL); Fenton & Lee used 75 dB(Z) noise bands, not speech.
- The 80-phon column in the K-minus-ear table sits at the edge of the standard's validity: the toolbox header says the contours are valid at all frequencies for 20 phon up to (not including) 80 phon, and at 5-12.5 kHz only up to 80 phon, so treat that column as indicative. — [IoSR toolbox iso226.m](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IoSR-Surrey/MatlabToolbox/master/%2Biosr/%2Bauditory/iso226.m)
- The ISO 226:2023 tables themselves were not read; I used the 2003 formula and arrays (2023 differs by at most 0.6 dB).
- BS.1770 does not state the stage corner frequencies in text; "+4 dB above about 1.7 kHz" and "38 Hz" come from the assignment and agree with my computed response.
- The sign convention for the positive errors in Fenton & Lee's table is my inference from their 63 Hz worked example.
- How K-weighting's rigid-sphere head shelf relates to headphone targets (diffuse-field or Harman, which already contain ear gain) was not researched; BS.1770's head model assumes loudspeaker listening.
- Time constants of the user's own Leveler and Cap are outside this research.

## 3. Why can a stream sit under a BS.1770 cap and still be unpleasant (sharpness, roughness, tonality, narrow-band energy versus critical-band loudness summation)?

### Takeaway
BS.1770 is a K-weighted energy sum, so it has no handle on spectral shape: sharpness (weighting rising above about 15-16 Bark, about 2.7-3 kHz), roughness (15-300 Hz envelope modulation, peak near 70 Hz), tonal prominence and critical-band loudness summation all differ between streams that read the same LKFS. In the psychoacoustic annoyance (PA) model the sharpness penalty starts only above 1.75 acum and the modulation terms weigh relatively more at low loudness, so harshness need not fall away as volume drops.

### Cited Findings
- Algorithm structure: K-weighting, mean-square per channel, channel-weighted sum, gating on 400 ms blocks (no critical-band analysis, compression nonlinearity or spectral-shape descriptor appears in the algorithm description). — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!MSW-E.docx)
- Sharpness (acum): 1 acum is a narrow-band noise within one critical band around 1 kHz at 60 dB; from 200 Hz to 3 kHz sharpness "increases slightly, but above 3 kHz strongly"; S = 0.11 x sum(L_B x g_B x B) / sum(L_B), with weighting g = 1 below 15 Bark and 0.066 e^(0.171 B) at and above it; "mostly independent of overall loudness, spectral centroid, or roughness". (Weak source: this is a music-timbre paper on Kachin versus Uyghur music, not a psychoacoustics paper; it cites Bismarck, Fastl and Zwicker for sharpness and states the weighting g as switching at 15 Bark, while the secondary DIN 45692 page below says 16.) — [Bader, Blaß & Franke, arXiv 2103.08203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08203)
- (secondary) DIN 45692 (2009) takes per-band specific loudness from Zwicker's model, normalizes the sum by total loudness, anchors 1 acum to 1 kHz narrow-band noise at 60 dB, uses a normalization constant between 0.105 and 0.115, and keeps the high-band weight at 1 up to 16 Bark then 0.066 e^(0.171 z); a 2025 Acta Acustica paper describes the weighting as rising from about 15 Bark (about 2.7 kHz); DIN lists three weighting variants (standard g, Aures gA, von Bismarck gB); perceived sharpness follows the spectral envelope rather than fine structure (2017 DAGA paper crediting Fastl and Zwicker). The standard's text itself was not accessed. — [MetricGate DIN 45692 page](https://metricgate.com/docs/sharpness-acum-din45692/), [search results incl. Acta Acustica and DAGA 2017](https://pub.dega-akustik.de/DAGA_2017/data/articles/000299.pdf)
- (computed from the cited weighting, with my Bark values) at 0.066 e^(0.171 z) the weight is 1.43 at 18 Bark, 2.02 at 20, 2.84 at 22 and 4.00 at 24; that is about 1.9 at 6 kHz (19.6 Bark) and about 2.5 at 8 kHz (21.3 Bark). The exponential reaches 1.0 at about 15.9 Bark, so the "16 Bark" switch is continuous while the "15 Bark" in the arXiv paper leaves a small step (0.86 at 15 Bark).
- Psychoacoustic annoyance: PA = N5 x (1 + sqrt(wS^2 + wFR^2)); wS = (S - 1.75) x 0.25 x log10(N5 + 10), active only above 1.75 acum; wFR = (2.18 / N5^0.4) x (0.4 F + 0.6 R); N5 is the loudness exceeded 5% of the time; roughness R (asper) peaks "near 70 Hz modulation rate"; fluctuation strength F (vacil) peaks "near 4 Hz"; "PA has no universal annoyance threshold"; the page notes its printed wS lacks a zero floor and that "at high loudness the relative contribution of modulation shrinks" (wFR carries N5^0.4 in the denominator). References: Zwicker & Fastl 1999; Fastl 2006. — [MetricGate, Psychoacoustic annoyance](https://metricgate.com/docs/psychoacoustic-annoyance/)
- (secondary) Roughness arises from envelope components of roughly 15-300 Hz, crests near 70 Hz for carriers at or above 1 kHz (lower carriers peak at slower rates) and, per Fastl & Zwicker, fluctuation strength peaks near 4 Hz; the asper unit is anchored to a 60 dB, 1 kHz tone fully modulated at 70 Hz; a calculator page links the 70 Hz peak to "unresolved" beating that registers as grainy and buzzy (not a scholarly source; no source covered voice). — [Fastl & Zwicker chapter](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-68888-4_11), [Salford acoustics](https://acoustics.salford.ac.uk/?p=716), [MetricGate roughness](https://metricgate.com/docs/roughness-asper/)
- (secondary) Loudness summation: with equal total energy, a sound spread over several critical bands is louder than one confined to one band; loudness stays flat while energy sits within one auditory filter and rises as the band widens; the cochlea pools energy over a critical region; critical-band concept supported for loudness summation (Zwicker 1957 and later work). Magnitudes in dB for given bandwidths were not retrieved. — [MetricGate loudness summation](https://metricgate.com/docs/loudness-summation-bandwidth/), [NYU lecture notes](https://www.cns.nyu.edu/~david/courses/perception/lecturenotes/loudness/loudness.html), [Zwicker abstracts (scite)](https://scite.ai/authors/eberhard-zwicker-2N8bxL)
- ECMA 418-2 (Sottek Hearing Model) separates tonal and noise loudness and the standardized models include an outer/middle-ear filter, an auditory filter bank and compressive nonlinearity; ISO 532-1 and 532-3 "overestimate the loudness of tonal components" between 1 and 5 kHz relative to ISO 226:2023. — [Sottek et al. 2023](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2023/data/articles/000585.pdf)

### Inferences
- A BS.1770 cap bounds K-weighted energy only. Streams with identical LKFS can differ in sharpness (energy above 3 kHz counted 1.9-2.5 times more per unit loudness in the 6-8 kHz sibilant band), in tonal prominence (a single harmonic on the 2.7 kHz peak) and in roughness (modulation near 70 Hz). Because the cap does not model critical-band summation, broadband sibilant bursts (3-8 kHz spans about 6 Bark by my numbers) are likely to be louder and sharper than a narrow vowel component with the same K-weighted energy; this is a model-level inference, not measured on speech.
- Sharpness is a ratio normalized by total loudness, so scaling the whole signal down leaves it unchanged to first order; only audibility changes (components falling below threshold drop out). That is the cleanest formal reason why spectral harshness persists "even when low".
- In the PA formula the modulation weight rises as N5 falls, so roughness/fluctuation matter relatively more in quiet playback; this is a property of the model for steady environmental noise and is unvalidated for speech through headphones.
- The deep-voice roughness idea in question 1 (F0 inside the 15-300 Hz roughness band) is consistent with these figures but untested for speech.
- -> in-plug-in researcher: sharpness, tonality or band-wise measures would be additions beyond the BS.1770 filter; cost and feasibility are not covered here.

### Gaps
- DIN 45692 itself, Zwicker & Fastl's book and Fastl's 2006 paper were not accessed; sharpness details come from a music-computation preprint and a calculator site, and the weighting onset is stated as 15 Bark in one and 16 Bark in the other.
- No sharpness, roughness or tonality measurements of female or male speech were found, and no published threshold for "unpleasant" sharpness in speech (PA's 1.75 acum onset is a model parameter for noise).
- Loudness-summation magnitudes (dB versus bandwidth) were not retrieved, nor how much summation matters at low level.
- The claim that unresolved harmonics cause the 70 Hz roughness peak rests on one non-scholarly page; sources also disagree on the upper edge of the roughness range (a 2025 conference paper appeared to give about 500 Hz versus about 300 Hz elsewhere; not opened).
- Tonality as a perceptual attribute of speech (as opposed to ECMA 418-2's tonal loudness) was not researched.

## 4. Which headphone and output-hardware factors add harshness or heaviness (peaks and resonances, closed-back seal and bass boost, Harman research, onboard output impedance)?

### Takeaway
Headphones add their own spectral shape that stays when the volume is turned down: an ear-gain region at 2-9 kHz (with narrow peaks near 8 kHz producing "grittiness" on S, F and T), a preference-driven bass lift (Harman-style shelf of about +6 dB below about 105-150 Hz that about one listener in five finds too heavy), seal-dependent bass for closed-backs, and a few dB of bass lift from a high output impedance such as the roughly 75 ohm typical of onboard audio.

### Cited Findings
- Ear gain: the page describes "a substantial elevation somewhere between 2-9khz" explained by the shape of the ear, which listeners' brains expect; headphones without it sound "muted and muffled"; Harman's ear-gain rise starts "around 1khz" and does not closely track the ear-related amplification; a "dip (deviation) somewhere between 9-10khz on most headphones" is attributed to the concha. — [headphones.com, Measurements and frequency response](https://headphones.com/pages/measurements-and-frequency-response)
- Treble peaks: a narrow 8 kHz peak can add "grittiness that shows up for consonant tones in vocals like 'S', 'F', and 'T'", only on some recordings; a wide, complementary shape (HiFiMAN Arya peaks near 4 kHz, about 8 kHz and 11 kHz) is smoother than narrow peaks; the suggested fix is a narrow EQ filter; the page's vocabulary is "sibilant", "peaky", "harshness", "grittiness", not "shrill". — [headphones.com, Measurements and frequency response](https://headphones.com/pages/measurements-and-frequency-response)
- Bass: the 2018 Harman target has "a substantial bass elevation below 150hz" (2013 version "more modest"); a shelf ending near 150 Hz sounds clearer than one running to 300 Hz, which can "sound kind of muddy and thick"; the lift is "there because we like it", not an ear-anatomy effect; the Sony WH-1000XM4 is the page's example of "boomy and overbearing bass"; diffuse-field targets are slightly brighter with less bass than Harman. — [headphones.com, Measurements and frequency response](https://headphones.com/pages/measurements-and-frequency-response)
- Harman numbers (over-ear beta target measured on the B&K 5128): "a 6.4dB shelf filter below 105Hz and a -1.4dB shelf filter above 2.5kHz" on top of the 5128 diffuse-field correction (the article does not sign the 6.4 dB value; presumably a boost); in a 13-listener pilot the plain diffuse-field curve scored lowest (21.2) against 70.8 for the Harman IE 2019 target and 64.2 for Harman Beta 2024; the author cautions against treating the ranking as definitive; the IEC 60318-4 pinna emphasizes highs 2-3 dB more than real ears. — [SoundGuys on Harman targets and the 5128](https://www.soundguys.com/harman-research-targets-the-bruel-kjaer-5128-112247)
- Preference research: the Harman target is "preferred by a majority (64%) of listeners"; "The Less Bass Is Better class (21% of listeners)" skews female and older with no trained listeners; "The More Bass Is Better class is skewed toward males versus females by a factor of 4 to 1"; no evidence of geographic influence; the target "is not the last word". — [Olive, Acoustics Today Spring 2022, p. 66](https://acousticstoday.org/issues/2022AT/Spring2022/files/basic-html/page66.html)
- (secondary) Closed-back seal: leaky fits lose low-end output below roughly 500 Hz (write-up of Olive's 2019 AES-LA talk); closed-back bass is tied to how well the headphone seals (PS Audio interview with Olive); test rigs with a tighter seal can inflate the bass listeners actually hear; RTINGS links bass loss to glasses frames and hair. Numbers are weak: a retail blog claims a gap of 1-2 mm can cost 3-5 dB below 100 Hz and a headphone site says leaks can differ "by tens of dB in severe cases"; no controlled glasses/hair measurement was found. — [AES section report](https://aes.org/sections/reports/?ID=3148), [PS Audio interview](https://www.psaudio.com/blogs/copper/engaging-and-enthralling-music-for-all-talking-with-dr-sean-olive-of-harman-international-part-one), [RTINGS](https://rtings.com/headphones/tests/sound-quality/frequency-response-consistency), [attackshark](https://attackshark.com/blogs/knowledges/earpads-acoustic-seal-sound-performance), [Headphonesty](https://www.headphonesty.com/2025/09/closed-back-headphones-detuning-hearing-headphone-engineer/)
- Output impedance: onboard motherboard audio averages "around 75 Ohms", phones 1-10 ohms, dongles and better amps about 1 ohm; worked example for a headphone listed at 100 ohms that is 300 ohms at 100 Hz and 100 ohms at 1 kHz, fed 3 V: 0.06 dB bass-over-mid shift from a 1 ohm source versus 2.94 dB from a 75 ohm source; the "1/8 rule" (source impedance at most one eighth of the headphone's) and "1 Ohm or less will be good enough"; multi-driver IEMs show "much, much more dramatic effects" (no numbers); planars with flat impedance are barely affected. — [headphones.com, Output impedance explained](https://headphones.com/blogs/features/the-most-important-spec-you-re-not-paying-attention-to-output-impedance-explained-11)
- (secondary) Realtek: one comparison lists the ALC1220 front-header stage at up to 2.1 Vrms with automatic load detection at 32, 150 and 600 ohms; no numeric output impedance for the jack was found and sources disagree on which jack carries the high-output stage; forum reports of onboard bass roll-off below roughly 100 Hz exist but are anecdotal. — [Igor's Lab onboard-audio guide](https://igorslab.de/en/disadvantages-of-onboard-sounds-problems-and-workarounds-headphones-guide-and-background-knowledge/8)
- Olive, Welti & McMullin (AES 2013): trained listeners rated an in-room loudspeaker-derived target highest on both headphones tested. (secondary abstract) — [AES e-library](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=16768)

### Inferences
- All of these are shape effects: they persist at low volume. A narrow peak near 8 kHz would hit high female voices harder (their sibilants sit higher), while seal or bass-lift variation matters most for deep voices; because fit changes each time headphones are put on, the effect can appear "sometimes".
- Because the ear-gain region is already in the response of many headphones, content with its own presence boost double-counts 2-5 kHz.
- At low playback level the ear is far less sensitive to bass (question 2), so a bass lift or poor seal is less likely than a treble peak to cause "too hard at low volume"; it would matter for the "heavy/pressing" quality of deep voices.
- The roughly 3 dB bass lift from onboard output impedance is small next to a 6 dB Harman shelf or seal variation and depends entirely on the headphone's impedance curve (unknown for the user's model).
- If the user is in the "less bass is better" class (21% in Olive's data; skews female and older), a Harman-like bass lift would sound "too heavy" on deep voices; this is a population statement, not a finding about the user. -> per-person tuning researcher.

### Gaps
- The user's headphone model, impedance curve, closed or open design, fit and the Realtek jack's real output impedance are unknown; this section is generic.
- No controlled measurement of how much glasses, hair or re-seating change bass was found; the 3-5 dB and "tens of dB" figures are weak.
- Olive & Welti's 2015 bass and treble preference paper and the 2016 leakage paper were not accessible (AES members only).
- The AES 2013 abstract and the Olive 2019 talk write-up are search-result summaries; the exact page behind the "500 Hz" statement was not opened.
- Which Harman variant the user's headphones were tuned to (if any) is unknown.

## 5. Which content factors (YouTube, podcasts, Zoom: compression, presence boosts, missing de-essing, noise suppression, codec artifacts, AGC, close-mic proximity effect) add harshness or heaviness?

### Takeaway
Content chains add spectral and temporal coloration that differs from talker to talker: proximity effect (ideal cardioid at 100 Hz: +21 dB at 1 inch, +6 dB at 6 inches), 5 kHz mic boosts and presence EQ that lift sibilance, and platform processing (Zoom noise suppression, echo cancellation and automatic gain, Opus/SILK codecs, YouTube Stable volume). This is the most direct explanation for the "sometimes" in the complaint.

### Cited Findings
- Proximity effect numbers: see question 1. — [cmtext, Studio gear ch. 2](https://cmtext.com/studio/chapter2_mics2.php)

Codec effects are not mentioned by this article. — [Sound On Sound, Managing sibilance](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/managing-sibilance)

## 6. Which listener factors (hyperacusis, recruitment, tinnitus-related sensitivity, auditory fatigue, misophonia) can make particular voices unpleasant at low level, and what do audiologists measure? (informational only, not diagnosis)

### Takeaway
Normal ear acoustics already amplify 2-5 kHz by roughly 15-20 dB with large individual variation, and clinical reduced sound tolerance (hyperacusis in its loudness, annoyance, fear and pain forms; recruitment with hearing loss; misophonia, which is pattern- or meaning-driven rather than loudness-driven) can make everyday sounds of "low-to-moderate intensity" aversive. Audiologists measure loudness discomfort levels (LDL/UCL) alongside the audiogram and questionnaires; a short list of red flags that warrant medical attention is given below.

### Cited Findings

**Definitions and presentation**
- Hyperacusis: people "experience the sounds of everyday life as intrusively loud, uncomfortable or even painful"; some "have particular sounds they find unpleasant whatever the volume" (fingernails on a chalkboard, a dripping tap); the point of maximum comfortable loudness "varies from person to person" and "will also change according to our mood and the context of the sound"; reduced tolerance is "especially likely to happen when you are tired or stressed"; common in autism spectrum disorder and with tinnitus; roughly 2% of adults have some degree of hyperacusis on a conservative estimate (an internet study suggested up to 9%, thought too high); hyperacusis is "usually associated with increased sensitivity (or auditory gain) in the central auditory system"; associated conditions include migraine, head injury, Lyme disease, Williams syndrome and Bell's palsy, sometimes ear surgery, and sudden loud noise can trigger it. — [Tinnitus UK, Hyperacusis leaflet (Baguley & McFerran, v4.0, Dec 2022)](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Tinnitus-UK-Hyperacusis-Leaflet.pdf)
- Descriptive definition: "a multifaceted condition wherein everyday sounds of low-to-moderate intensity" are "described as unbearably loud, aversive, painful, or some combination thereof"; primary subtypes are loudness, annoyance, fear and pain; there is "a lack of consensus on appropriate subjective and objective clinical protocols"; this is a single-author review, not a consensus paper. — [Jahn 2022, J Acoust Soc Am (PMC9448410)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9448410)
- (secondary) The four-subtype scheme (loudness, pain, annoyance, fear) comes from Tyler and colleagues' two-part review; the field has no single agreed definition. — [Tyler et al. 2014, Am J Audiol](https://doi.org/10.1044/2014_AJA-14-0010)
- Recruitment: "a physical condition of the inner ear that leads to reduced tolerance of loudness"; faint sounds are inaudible, then loudness grows "over-proportionally" once above threshold; it appears in the most impaired frequencies (the article places these at the high-pitched end); the leaflet's lay description: "from too little, to too much very quickly" ("Speak up a bit" then "Don't shout!"). — [Wikipedia, Recruitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recruitment_%28medicine%29), [Tinnitus UK leaflet](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Tinnitus-UK-Hyperacusis-Leaflet.pdf)
- Misophonia (Delphi consensus): "a disorder of decreased tolerance to specific sounds or stimuli associated with such sounds"; triggers are mostly sounds made by other people's bodies (eating, chewing, sniffing, throat-clearing, pen clicking, typing); responses "do not seem to be elicited by the loudness of auditory stimuli" and are tied to "the specific pattern or meaning to an individual"; reactions can resemble hyperacusis. — [Swedo et al. 2022, Front Neurosci](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.841816/full)
- Jastreboff's framework (secondary): decreased sound tolerance covers hyperacusis (reaction to physical characteristics of sound), misophonia (reactions to specific patterns or contexts), phonophobia, or a mixture. — [Frontiers Neurosci 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2023.895574/text)
- Hyperacusis versus tinnitus overlap: 381 hyperacusis patients, 86% also had tinnitus; 27.5% had normal thresholds in each ear; a 2023 study (secondary) of 40 patients and 47 controls found 95% distress, 82.5% anxiety or fear and 65% pain among patients. — [Sheldrake, Diehl & Schaette 2015, Front Neurol](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2015.00105/full), [Sci Rep 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-28570-8)

**Frequency pattern**
- LDLs of hyperacusis patients averaged about 85 dB HL (range 78-87) and were nearly flat from 0.5 to 4 kHz, against about 101-104 dB HL in a normal-hearing reference (a gap the text gives as 15.7-17.8 dB, though the paper's Table 1 means imply about 16.8-18.8 dB; the reference values were read from published graphs of 55 normal-hearing listeners, not tested as controls); at 6 kHz LDLs were nearly identical across the three hearing-loss groups. — [Sheldrake et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2015.00105/full)
- (secondary) In a 2023 Scientific Reports series LDLs were lower in patients at every frequency except 500 Hz, by roughly 1.4-6.1 dB HL (the abstract's parenthetical appears to conflict with the 500 Hz exception). — [Sci Rep 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-28570-8)
- Many hyperacusis patients have normal standard audiograms; standard-frequency sensitivity is "insufficient for diagnosing and monitoring"; one study found poorer thresholds than controls from 0.5 to 8 kHz even where averages were normal, another poorer 10-16 kHz thresholds despite normal standard-frequency results; loudness growth can be steeper (Brandy & Lynn 1995 at 1000 Hz) and elevated at mid-dynamic-range levels; a central-gain account is unproven in humans. — [Jahn 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9448410)

**What audiologists measure**
- LDLs ("the lowest intensity levels judged by an individual to be uncomfortably loud for a given stimulus") are "the only psychoacoustic test currently recommended for the differential diagnosis of hyperacusis" in the US, alongside questionnaires; cut-offs vary from under 95 to under 70 dB HL with "no consensus on the measurement technique"; LDLs alone "fail to capture the multifaceted nature of the hyperacusis condition"; extended high-frequency audiometry above 8 kHz is suggested when conventional testing is normal. — [Jahn 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9448410)
- Method in one clinic series: 0.5 s pure tones rising in 5 dB steps at 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 kHz until the patient signals discomfort; LDL "neither sensitive nor specific enough to serve as a single test"; LDLs correlate only moderately with thresholds (r = 0.36); LDLs below 70 dB HL are called "highly specific". — [Sheldrake et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fneur.2015.00105/full)
- A specialist (ENT or audiovestibular physician) asks when and how it started, other illnesses, hearing loss, noise exposure and tinnitus, may use mood, hyperacusis and quality-of-life questionnaires, examines the ears and arranges a hearing test. — [Tinnitus UK leaflet](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Tinnitus-UK-Hyperacusis-Leaflet.pdf)

**Auditory fatigue, exposure and red flags**
- Exposure: "long or repeated exposure to sounds at or above 85 dBA can cause hearing loss"; sounds at or below 70 dBA "even after long exposure, are unlikely to cause hearing loss"; headphones at top volume are listed at 94-110 dBA; loud noise can cause temporary hearing loss "that disappears 16 to 48 hours later" with possible residual damage; warning signs include sounds that are "distorted or muffled", trouble understanding speech and tinnitus. — [NIDCD, Noise-induced hearing loss](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/noise-induced-hearing-loss)
- Tinnitus red flags for onward referral: pulsatile tinnitus; tinnitus with significant vertigo; unilateral or asymmetric tinnitus; tinnitus with asymmetric hearing loss; tinnitus causing psychological distress; tinnitus with significant neurological symptoms or signs; all patients with the symptom should at least have an audiological assessment; about 14.4% of the population have tinnitus at any time. — [Tinnitus UK, Guidance for GPs v2.2 (Jan 2024)](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/Tinnitus-guidance-for-GPs-Ver-2.2-Tinnitus-UK.pdf)
- Sudden sensorineural hearing loss ("an unexplained, rapid loss of hearing either all at once or over a few days", frequently one ear, sometimes with ear fullness, dizziness or tinnitus) is "a medical emergency"; treatment delayed more than two to four weeks is less likely to reverse it. — [NIDCD, Sudden deafness](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness)
- If tolerance to sound is a concern, the leaflet advises discussing it with a GP, and notes severe cases can become so afraid of sound that they withdraw from daily activities. — [Tinnitus UK leaflet](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Tinnitus-UK-Hyperacusis-Leaflet.pdf)

**Anatomy and fatigue**
- Individual ear-acoustic differences: larger concha, pinna and canal openings tend to go with higher outer-ear gain, with variation above 2 kHz of up to about 20 dB (secondary). — [Goode](https://www.orl.uzh.ch/memro/program/3.9_Goode.pdf)
- Listening effort (secondary): degraded conferencing audio may raise effort and tiredness. — [arXiv 2202.01740](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.01740)

### Inferences
- A voice-specific, spectrum-dependent complaint ("some high female voices and some deep male voices, even at low volume, sometimes") is more consistent with a quality or frequency-pattern sensitivity than with generalized loudness hyperacusis, which would show for many everyday sounds and tends to give lower LDLs fairly evenly across 0.5-4 kHz. But the definition says hyperacusis concerns "low-to-moderate intensity" sounds, so "even when low" does not rule it out.
- Which pattern applies would be separated by observations an audiologist would elicit, not by this research: does the same unpleasantness occur with non-voice sounds in the same band (cutlery, rustling, alarms), with the same voice on other headphones or speakers, with certain talkers regardless of their spectrum (pattern or meaning, closer to misophonia), or with ear pain, tinnitus, fullness, dizziness or a change in hearing?
- If there is mild high-frequency hearing loss, recruitment can make 3-8 kHz content (sibilants, high-voice formants) grow too quickly above the raised threshold, so a modest level sounds harsh; this is inference from the recruitment description and needs an audiogram.
- Tolerance fluctuates with tiredness, stress and mood; the same voice can sound harsher at the end of a long listening or call session without any change in the signal.
- -> per-person tuning researcher: LDL-style self-calibration; -> protection-methods researcher: nothing here argues for a level-only fix.

### Gaps
- No study of voice-type- or sex-specific discomfort at low level was found; frequency-specific hyperacusis near 2-5 kHz is not established (the clinic series shows a nearly flat pattern).
- The NICE tinnitus guideline (NG155) could not be fetched (403), so its referral timeframes are not quoted; Baguley's and Jastreboff's primary articles and the Tyler review are seen only secondhand.
- No source retrieved on typical noise-induced notches at 3-6 kHz or on age-related high-frequency loss patterns; hidden hearing loss (cochlear synaptopathy) evidence was not retrieved (Jahn mentions only that 10-16 kHz thresholds can be poorer despite normal standard audiograms).
- Whether voices are common misophonia triggers by pitch was not found (the consensus text lists body sounds, not voice pitch).
- Typical temporary-threshold-shift recovery by frequency was seen only in a search-result abstract and is not cited here.

## 7. "Even when low": which causes depend on spectral shape regardless of level and which depend on level, and what are the likeliest causes and bands to watch for this user?

### Takeaway
Shape-dependent causes (excess 2-5 kHz presence and 3-8 kHz sibilance, proximity bass, headphone peaks and bass lift, processing artifacts, pattern-based aversion) persist when the volume slider goes down, while level-dependent causes (recruitment, hyperacusis loudness type, the size of the K-weighting error, exposure-related fatigue) mostly do not explain "hard even when low". Ranked for this user, the likeliest causes are source-side spectral coloration that differs by talker, the ear's own 2-5 kHz amplification, and the headphone's spectral shape.

### Cited Findings
- Shape properties that do not change with a level change: sharpness is a ratio normalized by total loudness (formula in question 3), and "mostly independent of overall loudness". — [Bader, Blaß & Franke, arXiv 2103.08203 (music-timbre paper; weak source)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08203)
- Misophonia responses are not loudness-driven. — [Swedo et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.841816/full)
- A narrow 8 kHz headphone peak adds grittiness on S, F and T on some recordings; proximity-effect boost is a property of mic distance and pattern (+21/+6/+3/<1 dB at 1/6/12/24 inches at 100 Hz, ideal cardioid). — [headphones.com](https://headphones.com/pages/measurements-and-frequency-response), [cmtext](https://cmtext.com/studio/chapter2_mics2.php)
- Level-dependent properties: equal-loudness contours change with level (computed in question 2: 100 Hz is 28.4 dB less sensitive than 1 kHz at 20 phon but 12.5 dB less at 80 phon, while 3.15 kHz is +5.7 dB at 20 phon versus +2.9 dB at 80 phon). — [IoSR toolbox iso226.m](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IoSR-Surrey/MatlabToolbox/master/%2Biosr/%2Bauditory/iso226.m), [Suzuki et al. 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast/45/1/45_e23.66/_pdf)
- Hyperacusis is defined by reactions to "low-to-moderate intensity" sounds, recruitment is abnormal loudness growth above an elevated threshold, and tolerance drops when tired or stressed. — [Jahn 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9448410), [Wikipedia, Recruitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recruitment_%28medicine%29), [Tinnitus UK leaflet](https://tinnitus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Tinnitus-UK-Hyperacusis-Leaflet.pdf)
- PA weights: modulation term proportional to N5^-0.4 (relatively larger when quiet); sharpness term needs more than 1.75 acum. — [MetricGate PA](https://metricgate.com/docs/psychoacoustic-annoyance/)
- Exposure thresholds for damage (85 dBA; 70 dBA unlikely) are level-based and do not describe discomfort at low level. — [NIDCD](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/noise-induced-hearing-loss)

### Inferences

#### Shape-dependent versus level-dependent

| Cause | Persists when volume is turned down (shape)? | Level dependence |
|---|---|---|
| Excess 2-5 kHz presence, F3/F4 energy, narrow tonal harmonics near 2.7 kHz | Yes (shape unchanged; the ear's 3-4 kHz advantage is a bit larger at low level) | Weak |
| Sibilance 3-8 kHz, sharpness | Yes (sharpness is normalized by loudness) | Weak; components below threshold drop out |
| Narrow headphone peak (about 8 kHz) | Yes | Weak |
| Proximity bass, headphone bass lift, seal variation | Shape yes, but audibility of bass falls at low level (100 Hz: -28 dB re 1 kHz at 20 phon vs -12.5 dB at 80 phon) | Strong (less audible when quiet) |
| Codec, noise-suppression, AGC, Stable volume artifacts | Yes (signal-domain) | None |
| Roughness / fluctuation | Yes; PA modulation weight relatively larger when quiet | Weak |
| Misophonia-type pattern or meaning sensitivity | Yes (not loudness-driven) | None |
| Hyperacusis loudness/annoyance/pain/fear subtypes | Partly; defined at low-to-moderate intensity | Varies by subtype |
| Recruitment with high-frequency loss | Frequency-specific; loudness grows quickly above an elevated threshold | Strong |
| Fatigue, tiredness, stress | History-dependent | Yes |
| K-weighting error size | Not a cause of harshness; error changes with level (bass over-credit larger when quiet) | Yes |

#### Ranked likeliest causes for this user (judgment from the evidence above, not a measurement; the user's own streams and headphone were not analyzed)

1. **Source-side spectral coloration that differs by talker.** Close-mic proximity bass build-up (deep voices) and bright-mic, 5 kHz-boost, presence-EQ and sibilance (high voices), plus recording distortion and platform processing. Fits "sometimes", both voice types and "even when low". Mechanism well sourced (questions 1 and 5); not measured on the user's streams. Likelihood: high.
2. **The ear's own amplification of 2-5 kHz.** Outer-ear gain about 15-20 dB peaking near 2.7 kHz and a +4 to +6 dB pure-tone advantage at 3-4 kHz at 20-40 phon (computed) make any excess in that band the most salient at low level while bass excess fades; high voices put F3, F4 and several harmonics exactly there. Not a fault, but the amplifier of cause 1. Likelihood: high.
3. **Headphone spectral shape.** Treble peaks (a narrow peak near 8 kHz gives grit on S/F/T), the 2-9 kHz ear-gain region, bass lift (Harman-style shelf, leaky versus sealed closed-back) and a few dB from onboard output impedance on some headphones. Level-independent and varies with fit ("sometimes"). Model unknown, so likelihood: medium-high.
4. **Qualities a loudness cap cannot see.** Sharpness (weight 1.9 at 6 kHz and 2.5 at 8 kHz, computed), tonal prominence, roughness, and no critical-band loudness summation: this is why a stream under the cap can still be unpleasant rather than a separate acoustic source. Formula-level evidence only. Likelihood: medium (as the reason protection misses it).
5. **Dynamics and artifacts added by the platform.** AGC, noise-suppression pumping, codec artifacts, Stable volume raising transient or sibilant prominence. Processing is documented; perceptual effect unmeasured. Likelihood: medium-low.
6. **Listener-side sensitivity.** Hyperacusis subtypes, recruitment with high-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus-related sensitivity, misophonia-type pattern sensitivity, fatigue. Low prior for any individual (about 2% conservative estimate for hyperacusis) but the largest consequence and checkable by an audiologist; more likely if the discomfort also occurs with non-voice sounds or other headphones, with ear pain, tinnitus, fullness, dizziness or hearing change (see red flags in question 6). Likelihood: unknown, low to medium.
7. **Output-impedance bass lift.** About 3 dB in the cited example; unknown for the user's pair. Likelihood: low.
8. **K-weighting mis-weighting as a direct cause.** Unlikely: within about 3 dB at 2-4 kHz, over-credits bass at low level (question 2). Likelihood: low.

### Gaps
- No study directly tests voice-type-specific discomfort at low playback level; the ranking is judgment built on mechanism-level evidence, and the two voice types may need separate causes (treble-led for high voices, bass/presence-led for deep voices).
- The user's actual streams, headphone response, Realtek output impedance and hearing status were not analyzed, so the ranking is generic; the headphone model is the single most useful missing fact.
