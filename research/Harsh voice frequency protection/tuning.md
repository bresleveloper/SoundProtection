## Key Question 1: How do audiologists measure loudness discomfort per frequency (LDL/UCL; Cox Contour Test), and how do NAL-NL2 and DSL v5 turn it into per-channel maximum-output limits?

### Takeaway
Clinics find the lowest level a listener calls "too loud" with an ascending run at set frequencies, or map seven loudness categories (Cox Contour), and repeat measurements differ by about 5-6 dB. Fittings then turn that into a ceiling: DSL v5 limits aided speech so that speech peaks stay under the listener's ULC and checks maximum output with a narrowband signal, while NAL-NL2 deliberately prescribes maximum output from thresholds, not from measured discomfort. Standard clinical starting levels can already exceed a sensitive listener's limit, and narrowband results overstate what broadband speech tolerates.

### Cited Findings
**Measuring discomfort**
- The loudness discomfort level (LDL) is framed as "the intensity at which an auditory signal elicits an unfavorable subjective response" (Gagne and colleagues 1991, drawing on Hawkins 1980) — [Gagne, Seewald & Zelisko 1991, JSLPA](https://cjslpa.ca/files/1991_JSLPA_Vol_15/No_04_1-80/Gagne_Seewald_Zelisko_JSLPA_1991.pdf) (search summary; the PDF is a scan I could not read).
- Pascoe's procedure is an ascending method of limits inside a one-interval forced-choice format, and the LDL is the lowest level the listener calls "too loud" — same search as the Gagne item, [same PDF link](https://cjslpa.ca/files/1991_JSLPA_Vol_15/No_04_1-80/Gagne_Seewald_Zelisko_JSLPA_1991.pdf) (search summary; I could not tie the sentence to one page).
- The search found no ASHA or BSA step-by-step LDL text (stimulus type, step size, wording, maximum level). The BSA publishes the ULL method as a standalone 2011 procedure, "Determination of Uncomfortable Loudness Levels", for which no official copy turned up; the official file that was found is the pure-tone audiometry procedure, [BSA Recommended Procedure, Pure-Tone Audiometry 2018](https://www.thebsa.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Recommended-Procedure-Pure-Tone-Audiometry-2018.pdf) (search summary; not read).
- Aazh & Moore studied 362 patients: 21% had BSA-based pure-tone presentation levels above their uncomfortable loudness level (ULL) at one or more frequencies other than 1 kHz (1 kHz was tested first); 24% had the BSA-recommended 60 dB HL ULL starting level above their ULL; the authors conclude starting levels "should be lower" for this patient group — [Aazh & Moore 2017, Am J Audiol 26(3):226-232](https://doi.org/10.1044/2017_AJA-17-0011) (abstract only).
- A CPD summary of that work: a 15 dB HL start would cut audiometric discomfort to about 0.5% versus 3.6% at 30 dB HL, and the group advocated starting at 0 dB HL at 1 kHz, each later frequency beginning at the previous threshold — [CPD UK summary](https://cpduk.co.uk/news/discomfort-for-patients-with-tinnitus-and-or-hyperacusis) (search summary).
- Normal-hearing listeners reach about 100 dB HL on average at ULL testing; an across-frequency average of 77 dB HL or lower in the more sensitive ear is a proposed hyperacusis criterion; the most extreme cases are near 10 dB HL, which makes discomfort during testing more likely — [CPD UK: whisper-level hyperacusis](https://cpduk.co.uk/news/severe-hyperacusis-patients-who-find-the-sound-level-of-a-whisper-uncomfortably-loud) and [HRA study summary](https://www.hra.nhs.uk/planning-and-improving-research/application-summaries/research-summaries/pattern-of-uncomfortable-loudness-levels-in-hyperacusis/) (search summary; the statement came from one of the pages in that result set).
- Repeatability: 11 normal-hearing adults aged 39-73 were tested on 8-10 occasions across nearly a year; LDLs were "stable across sessions", varying by roughly 5 dB — [Hawley et al. 2017, PMID 28286362](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28286362) (abstract only, read through the Europe PMC search API).

**Cox Contour Test and categorical scaling**
- The Contour Test returns a sound level for each of seven loudness categories, from "very soft" to "uncomfortably loud" (the abstract names only the two ends); norms came from 45 normal-hearing listeners with warble tones (six frequencies) and broadband speech, and reliability from 10 hearing-impaired listeners retested at two frequencies across two sessions; most test-retest differences were 6 dB or less — [Cox, Alexander, Taylor & Gray 1997, Ear Hear 18(5):388-400](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004) (abstract only).
- In the same study the average category levels in dB HL did not vary across warble-tone frequencies (so the frequencies were pooled), warble-tone loudness growth differed slightly in shape from speech, and increment size mattered only for older hearing-impaired listeners (larger increments raised category levels; moderate changes had no significant effect in young normal-hearing listeners) — [Cox et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004) (abstract only).
- The authors caution against using narrowband (warble-tone) results blindly to prescribe aids, because loudness increases as bandwidth widens — [Cox et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004) (abstract only).
- A Contour scoring sheet runs 0-120 dB in 5 dB steps — [Contour Test listing](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/50790605) (search summary). The Cox abstract says test procedures and scoring forms for manual use are downloadable at www.ausp.memphis.edu/harl — [Cox et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004) (abstract only).
- A Purdue dissertation with 62 hearing-impaired listeners found most test-retest shifts within +-5 dB, with large differences between listeners — [Purdue AAI9622820](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI9622820) (search summary).
- ISO 16832:2006 categorical loudness scaling, as implemented by Fultz et al.: 11 categories (0-50 in steps of 5; seven labelled "Can't Hear", "Very Soft", "Soft", "Medium", "Loud", "Very Loud", "Too Loud"), pure tones at 1 and 4 kHz, one ear at a time through ER3A inserts, levels 0-110 dB SPL — [Fultz et al. 2020, Front Psychol 11:578352](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.578352/full).
- Same paper: listeners were told to answer "Too Loud" if the sound was loud enough that they "wouldn't want to hear it again"; after a "Too Loud" the upper limit for the next run was reduced by 5 dB, so the uncomfortable level is not presented again; the Discussion presents avoiding such levels as a benefit for people with hyperacusis — [Fultz et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.578352/full).
- Same paper: the fixed-level method used up to 22 levels at 5 dB spacing with ten presentations each (up to 220 trials, about 15 min per frequency); the slope-adaptive method averaged 2 min 47 s and the maximum-expected-information method 2 min 35 s, all in a sound-treated research room; the authors say categorical loudness scaling has "not been accepted by clinicians, partly due to the time required" — [Fultz et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.578352/full).

**Hearing-aid fittings**
- NAL-NL2: "We prescribe maximum output of the hearing aid from hearing thresholds"; "We are still prescribing MPO based on threshold, not on measurement of discomfort level"; about 12 years earlier NAL researched loudness discomfort and found it "did not add enough to the prescription in order to justify the time taken clinically"; on multichannel aids "We think we allowed too aggressively"; and a too-loud check acts on gain ("we simply turn the gain down at all frequencies") — [Keidser & Dillon, AudiologyOnline Q&A, 22 Oct 2012](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/siemens-expert-series-nal-nl2-11355).
- The Keidser et al. 2011 NAL-NL2 paper describes gain optimisation and compression and contains no maximum-output method — [Keidser et al. 2011, Audiol Res 1(1):e24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4627149/).
- The earlier NAL saturation-output target is the midpoint between a lower limit (avoid saturating on speech; from the gain a loss needs with speech at 75 dB SPL overall) and an upper limit (avoid discomfort; LDL measured or estimated from thresholds), predicted from the 500/1000/2000 Hz average: 89 dB SPL for normal hearing, 107 at 60 dB HL, 139 at 120 dB HL; a separate value per frequency is an alternative, and the value is lowered for multichannel aids that limit per band — [Dillon & Storey 1998, Ear Hear 19(4):255-266](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199808000-00001) (abstract only; also [MQ record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/the-national-acoustic-laboratories-procedure-for-selecting-the-sa/)).
- DSL v5: "the targets for aided speech are limited so that speech peaks do not exceed the listener's ULC"; the limit is called Broadband Output Limiting Thresholds (BOLT); a BOLT-limited target is identifiable by "a target LTASS that parallels the ULC contour, and is exactly 13 dB below the ULC"; speech peaks are "typically about 10 or 12 dB above the LTASS level at each frequency"; limiting occurs mostly at higher input levels or severe losses — [Scollie, AudiologyOnline, 15 Jan 2007](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/dsl-version-v5-0-description-959).
- Same article: the maximum-output (MPO) check uses a narrowband stimulus at 85 dB SPL in the real ear or 90 dB SPL in the coupler, and the new MPO target "will be somewhat lower than ULC for most hearing losses"; the article does not say whether the ULC is measured or predicted from thresholds, and does not say outright that the cap is frequency-specific — [Scollie 2007](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/dsl-version-v5-0-description-959).

**Questionnaires (informational only; not used for diagnosis)**
- The Khalfa Hyperacusis Questionnaire has 14 items (maximum 42); cut-offs of 28 (Khalfa 2002) and 26 (Meeus 2010) are quoted; Aazh & Moore recommend a dual threshold of ULLmin 77 dB HL or lower with a score of 22 or higher, which agree for about 95% of patients — [BAA poster](https://www.baaudiology.org/app/uploads/2020/11/New-Hyperacusis-Q-poster.pdf) and [PMC6122267](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6122267) (search summary; primary papers and item wording not read).

### Inferences
- **Borrow the procedure, not the numbers (DESIGN).** An ascending run that stops at the first "too hard", 5 dB steps, two runs keeping the lower result, and a repeat when the two differ by more than 6 dB (Cox's retest spread). Finer steps than 5 dB would claim precision that the measurement does not have.
- **DSL gives the two-step template (DERIVED from the cited numbers).** First a narrowband limit (ULC-like), then an offset for running speech: BOLT puts long-term speech 13 dB under the ULC because peaks sit 10-12 dB over the long-term level. Our band detector reads short-term band power (5 ms class, `implementation.md` Key Question 4), so it reads speech nearer its peaks than its long-term average. A ceiling set at the level where a steady tone becomes "too hard" will therefore only bite on a voice whose short-term band peaks reach it, and a voice's long-term band level can sit about 10 dB lower without triggering it (the Byrne gap, Key Question 4). A ceiling meant to catch typical voices has to be set lower, or taken from the voice-based test (Key Question 3, test 2).
- **NAL's finding argues for few bands and a global net.** Measured discomfort "did not add enough" for typical fittings and multichannel limiting was over-allowed; so start with the two or three bands that the data point to, and keep the broadband Safety Cap as the backstop rather than relying on many per-band limits to add up safely.
- **Narrowband results are upper bounds for voices (DESIGN).** Loudness grows with bandwidth (Cox's caution), and voices are broadband, so a ceiling taken from one narrow band is too high when several bands are loud at once; check with voice clips before trusting it.
- **Measured discomfort is the only individual data available here (DESIGN).** NAL's stated reason for skipping measured LDL is that it "did not add enough to the prescription" to justify the clinical time; Ariel has no audiogram in the project files, so a measured "too hard" level is the only personal evidence. It is not clinical: the scale is dBFS through an unknown headphone, so the dB HL norms (77 dB HL criterion, 100 dB HL average) cannot be applied to it.
- **The pattern across frequency has a normal shape (UNCERTAIN).** Cox found category levels in dB HL were the same across frequencies, so in a normal ear "too loud" follows the threshold curve. On our scale a band whose "too hard" level sits well under what that pattern predicts would suggest extra sensitivity or a headphone resonance, but without an SPL calibration the two cannot be separated.

### Gaps
- The names of the Contour categories between "very soft" and "uncomfortably loud" are not in the abstract; a search summary guessed them from memory ("comfortable but slightly soft" and so on), unverified. The forms at www.ausp.memphis.edu/harl were not retrieved.
- The BSA ULL procedure text (stimulus, step size, instructions, maximum level) and ASHA's own LDL guidance were not obtained; one search summary says a 2025 conference paper amends the BSA ULL protocol slightly, text cut off.
- Khalfa et al. 2002 and the Aazh & Moore dual-threshold paper were read only through search summaries; item wording and scoring rules are unverified.
- DSL v5: whether the ULC is measured or predicted is not stated in the article; Scollie et al. 2005 and Sherlock & Formby 2005 (normative LDLs) were not obtained.
- No source gives LDL or "too hard" values per band for headphone listening in dBFS or dB SPL.
- No quantitative source on how narrowband limits combine for broadband speech beyond Cox's caution.

## Key Question 2: What do the consumer self-tests and personalisation tools (Apple Headphone Accommodations, Mimi, SoundID, Samsung Adapt Sound, Nura) test and adjust?

### Takeaway
None of them measures discomfort per band or sets a ceiling. They measure what is audible (Mimi, Samsung, Nura, SoundID's hearing test) or what is preferred (Apple, SoundID) and then boost or reshape; no page found mentions loudness discomfort or limiting loud sound.

### Cited Findings
- Apple Headphone Accommodations, guided setup: "You'll listen to audio samples and choose which ones sound better to you", then you apply the suggested settings; manual tone and amplification can be changed while playing a sample; the page does not say whether the samples are tones or speech, how many there are, or which frequencies change, and says nothing about loud sounds, volume caps or limiting — [Apple Support HT211218](https://support.apple.com/HT211218).
- Apple, manual options: "Tune Audio For" offers Balanced Tone, Vocal Range and Brightness; the amplification control (Level on iPhone and iPad, Optimization on Mac) is Slight, Moderate or Strong and sets how much soft sounds are boosted; no amounts are given — [Beats User Guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/beats/dev2a32d841d/web).
- Mimi masked-threshold test: a pure tone sweeps 3.2-6 kHz against a fixed noise masker centred at 4 kHz with 320 Hz bandwidth; the listener adjusts the tone level; the result is the softest audible tone in the presence of the masker; "we estimate your general hearing profile rather than the standard dB HL audiogram"; the profile is stored on the headphone; the processing is not named; the test "does not constitute medical advice or diagnosis" — [Mimi blog](https://mimi.io/blog/how-to-interpret-your-hearing-test-results-mt-test-for-sound-personalization) (vendor page).
- Samsung Adapt Sound: tones alternate between ears and you answer yes or no for each; the feature "tries to determine which frequencies to boost or tone down"; it can apply to calls or to calls and media; the article says nothing about loudness discomfort, caps or limiting — [SamMobile, 10 June 2022](https://www.sammobile.com/news/one-ui-tip-get-the-best-audio-quality-adapt-sound/) (secondary; menu names may have changed since 2022).
- Samsung Galaxy Buds hearing test and hearing-aid mode: roughly five minutes of pure-tone audiometry per ear, NAL-NL2 amplification, FDA authorisation as an over-the-counter hearing aid for adults with perceived mild-to-moderate loss, rollout slated for Q4 2026 — [Wareable](https://www.wareable.com/samsung/samsung-galaxy-buds-3-pro-4-pro-otc-hearing-test-aids-amplification) and [Wearable Technologies](https://wearable-technologies.com/cn/news/fda-clears-galaxy-buds-to-function-as-hearing-aids) (search summary; secondary).
- SoundID: a preference test (choose one of two correction types) plus a hearing test of each ear; correction 1 adjusts for how far your hearing departs from the norm using ISO 7029:2017 age data ("you hear music as your peers do"), correction 2 equalises both ears to one baseline; EQ and compression are not mentioned, and neither is any limiting or volume cap; the page says SoundID "does not replace medical treatment" — [Sonarworks blog](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/research/soundid-personalization-tailor-your-sound-and-stay-on-top-of-your-hearing-health) (vendor page).
- Nura: the headphones play a set of frequencies into the ear and log the otoacoustic echo; the profile is stored on the device and playback is reshaped with some bands lifted and others cut; one site quoting the company's website gives roughly 60 s for a reading, and HearingTracker notes that a Kickstarter pitch framed it as about 30 s; HearingTracker calls it a crude picture of thresholds that struggles beyond mild loss, and Nura's support page says it does not fully serve people with specific hearing needs including tinnitus; which frequencies are tested is not explained — [HearingTracker](https://www.hearingtracker.com/de/stories/news/nura-claims-its-headphones-automatically-measure-your-hearing) and [Nura help](https://help.nuraphone.com/en/Will-the-Nuraphone-NuraLoop-or-NuraTrue-work-if-I-have-specific-hearing-needs-e4e7) (search summary; secondary).

### Inferences
- All of these estimate thresholds or preferences and then add gain or shape the spectrum. Ariel's problem is the opposite side (too much), where only the audiology discomfort procedures (Key Question 1) apply, so the in-app test should borrow its structure from LDL and categorical scaling, not from these apps.
- Apple's "which sounds better" step and Mimi's level adjustment show that short paired comparisons are an accepted consumer interaction, so an A/B step in the tray is plausible; but their output is a static EQ or gain, not a dynamic per-band ceiling.
- Samsung's use of NAL-NL2 means a threshold-prescribed maximum output (Key Question 1), again not measured discomfort.
- The absence of any mention of loud-sound limiting on these pages is not proof that the products do nothing there; the pages are simply silent (`protection.md` Key Question 5 covers what consumer features really do).

### Gaps
- Mimi's processing (EQ versus multiband compression), the content of Apple's guided samples, SoundID's test frequencies and duration, and Nura's test frequencies are not published on the pages read.
- Samsung's hearing-aid authorisation and the Q4 2026 rollout rest on secondary reports; no first-party Samsung page was read.
- The Apple page read (HT211218) does not name the tone presets; the Beats guide does, so the two Apple sources describe slightly different surfaces and may not match every device.
- No independent evaluation of any of these tools for a "too much" complaint was found.

## Key Question 3: What would a safe self-test inside the tray app look like (stimuli, levels, bursts, recording "too hard" per band, fatigue and order effects), given what the tray can already play, capture and show?

### Takeaway
The tray today can play one fixed 1 kHz sine and can read meters, volume, per-app peaks and plug-in telemetry; it has no loopback capture, no filters, no noise, no spectrum and no response UI, so the "WASAPI loopback sampler" in the brief does not exist in the code. A safe self-test is an ascending, stop-at-first-"too hard" run of short ramped bursts in 5 dB steps, never above the cap in K-weighted terms, with randomised band order, anchors and rests, plus a second, relative test (one band raised in a real voice, level fixed) because "hard even when low" is about spectral shape.

**Method sources for the protocol**
- Ascending run that stops at the first "Too Loud", levels 5 dB apart, a 5 dB lower upper limit after a "Too Loud", and a retest spread of 6 dB or less — [Fultz et al. 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.578352/full) and [Cox et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004) (abstract only for Cox; details in Key Question 1).
- Standard clinical starting levels can exceed a sensitive listener's ULL (21% and 24% of 362 patients at different steps) — [Aazh & Moore 2017](https://doi.org/10.1044/2017_AJA-17-0011) (abstract only; details in Key Question 1).
- After sequences of lower-level tones at one frequency (f1) and higher-level tones at another (f2), f1 tones "appear relatively louder" than f2 tones; exposure to only the higher-level tones gives similar shifts; the recalibration is "substantial" in the exposed ear and "smaller but still clearly evident" in the other ear; the abstract gives no dB values — [Marks 1994, ASA abstract 4aPP14](https://auditory.org/asamtgs/asa94mit/4aPP/4aPP14.html) (abstract only).
- For continuous tones at 0.125-16 kHz, 5-40 dB SL, over a 6 min exposure: "Most of the adaptation occurred within the first 3 min", with larger declines at low sensation levels and above 8 kHz — [Hellman, Miskiewicz & Scharf 1996, ASA abstract 2pPP7](https://auditory.org/asamtgs/asa96ind/2pPP/2pPP7.html) (abstract only).
- The long-term average speech spectrum (LTASS) in one-third-octave bands, for a speech-shaped noise reference, is tabulated in Byrne et al. 1994, JASA 96:2108-2120 — [MQ record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/an-international-comparison-of-long-term-average-speech-spectra/) (search summary; values not retrieved).
- The ear is most sensitive between 2 and 5 kHz — [Wikipedia: Equal-loudness contour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal-loudness_contour).

### Inferences
**Level bookkeeping (DERIVED from `native\dsp\SpDsp.h` lines 151-179).** For a sound that is identical in both channels, BS.1770 loudness is `LUFS = dBFS peak of a sine + K(f) - 0.691`, where K(f) is the K-weighting gain at the tone's frequency (mono would read 3.01 dB lower). The same holds for narrowband noise expressed in the plug-in's band unit (dB re full-scale sine, `implementation.md` Key Question 4). Computed at 48 kHz from the constants in the file:

| Frequency | K(f) re 0 dB | LUFS minus sine peak | Sine peak that reads -20 LUFS |
|---|---|---|---|
| 125 Hz | -0.73 dB | -1.42 dB | -18.6 dBFS |
| 250 Hz | -0.15 dB | -0.84 dB | -19.2 dBFS |
| 500 Hz | +0.04 dB | -0.65 dB | -19.4 dBFS |
| 1000 Hz | +0.70 dB | +0.01 dB | -20.0 dBFS |
| 2000 Hz | +3.07 dB | +2.38 dB | -22.4 dBFS |
| 4000 Hz | +3.97 dB | +3.28 dB | -23.3 dBFS |
| 8000 Hz | +4.04 dB | +3.35 dB | -23.4 dBFS |

Consequence: equal digital peak is not equal K-weighted loudness. The "never above the cap" limit for band `f` is `peak <= cap - K(f) + 0.691`, and it moves with the cap. The tray must therefore specify stimulus levels in K-weighted terms (a C# copy of the K-weighting, or a calibration table made with the native code) and report results both ways: as K-weighted level and as band level in the plug-in's `ceilingDb` unit.

**Safety rails (DESIGN).**
- Run only when telemetry shows the plug-in alive, `Enabled` and not `SafeMode`, and the watched output is the protected Headphones; otherwise refuse, because the cap would not be enforced.
- The highest burst level is the current cap in K-weighted terms (read from the settings before every burst, so a change mid-test is honoured). A band whose "too hard" level is not reached by then is recorded as "not reached within the cap": the cap already protects that band.
- Start well under the cap (for example 30 dB under; a tuning choice), ascend in 5 dB steps, stop at the first "too hard", and never present the level that was "too hard" again (Fultz: next run's upper limit 5 dB lower).
- Short bursts with the existing 50 ms ramps; one global Stop key; the tray keeps only reading the slider and records it with every burst (`VolumeDb`), and abandons the session if the slider rises during the test.
- One-ear presentations, if added, must keep each ear's level at or under the diotic limit; a one-ear signal reads 3 dB lower on the Leveler's meter than the same digital level in both ears (the meter sums the channels' power), so the cap check alone would let the single ear receive more.
- Pause other audio first (the tray already knows which apps are playing), so the telemetry slots belong to the test. After each burst read the Leveler slot's `gainReductionDb`; any reduction means the delivered level differs from the requested one, so mark the trial invalid.
- Tell Ariel what the test is, what stops it, and that sounds that hurt or any sudden change in hearing end the session and call for a clinician (Key Question 6).

**Stimuli (DESIGN; the brief's four stimulus types plus two options).**
- *Band-limited noise* (white or pink through a band-pass with the same centre and Q the plug-in will use). Then the test level and the plug-in detector read in one unit. Q 1.4 is about an octave wide (octave band: f0 divided by bandwidth is 1.414; DERIVED). Noise level in the band unit is its rms plus 3.01 dB.
- *Warble or FM tone* at the band centre (Cox used warble tones; NAudio has no warble generator, so build it on `ToneGenerator`). Its peak is its band level, which makes level bookkeeping exact.
- *Speech-shaped noise* (LTASS shape) as a neutral broadband reference and as the check that several bands together are not "too hard" when each alone is fine.
- *Real voice clips with one band raised*: a `PeakingEQ` at the plug-in's centre and Q, +3, +6, +9, +12 dB, then renormalised to the same K-weighted loudness as the unmodified clip so only the spectral shape changes (test 2 below).
- *A/B pairs*: the reference clip and the raised clip, order randomised, answer "which is harder?" (counts judged with the binomial table in Key Question 5).
- *Optional slow sweep* (`SignalGenerator` Sweep) at a fixed low level, with a "hard here" key, as a quick map before the band tests.

**Two tests, because the complaint does not depend on level (DESIGN; UNCERTAIN until Ariel's usual slider position is known).**
- *Test 1, absolute (LDL-style).* Per band, ascending noise or warble bursts up to the cap, giving `T`, the lowest K-weighted level called "too hard". The cap acts before the slider, so at a low slider the digital level can sit at the cap while the ear level is low. If Ariel still finds voices hard there, test 1 will end "not reached within the cap" for every band, which is itself informative: the complaint is not about level.
- *Test 2, relative (shape).* At Ariel's usual slider and a fixed long-term K-weighted level a few dB under the cap (speech's momentary loudness swings above its long-term value, and a swing over the cap makes the Leveler act, which invalidates the trial; repeat about 10-15 dB lower, the "even when low" condition, to see whether tolerance to the boost depends on level, which `cause.md` Key Question 7 separates from shape), play several real voices (one high female, one deep male, one mid) unmodified and with one band raised, ascending; stop at the first "too hard"; include unmodified clips as catch trials. The result is the largest boost still "fine". It maps to the relative threshold in `implementation.md` Key Question 4 (`L_band - L_wideband > R`), measured with the plug-in's own detector offline (not assumed equal to the nominal boost, because the wideband level changes too).
- A two-question variant in early sessions (on the same bursts, "too loud?" and "too hard?") shows whether Ariel's "hard" is loudness or a quality; if they differ, the LDL machinery is only a partial guide.

**Procedure per band (DESIGN).** Run 1 ascends from the start level to the first "too hard" (`T1`) or to the cap. Run 2 re-tests only up to 5 dB under `T1`; if "too hard" appears there, `T2` is that level and the lower of the two is kept; otherwise `T1` stands. If `T1` and `T2` differ by more than 6 dB, repeat on another occasion. Catch trials (silence, and a clearly quiet burst) appear now and then; a "too hard" on a catch trial flags a false alarm and a need for rest.

**Response capture (DESIGN).** Three answers per burst on the keyboard: "fine", "too hard", "can't hear", plus Stop. The instruction wording follows ISO 16832 as used by Fultz: too hard if it is hard enough that you would not want to keep listening. Log per burst: time, band, stimulus type, requested level (K-weighted and band unit), cap, slider dB, ear level (`EarLevel`), telemetry gain reduction, answer, reaction time.

**Order, fatigue and context effects (DESIGN, from the cited studies).**
- Randomise band order within a session and counterbalance across sessions, because recent level history shifts loudness judgements per frequency: after quieter tones at f1 and louder tones at f2, f1 tones appear relatively louder than f2 tones (Marks 1994).
- Use bursts with silence between them, never long continuous tones, because adaptation for continuous tones is mostly within the first 3 min (Hellman 1996) and fixed-level categorical scaling is long (Fultz).
- Interleave a fixed anchor (1 kHz at a comfortable level well under the cap) every few bursts; if its rating moves from "fine" toward "too hard", end the session and rest.
- Keep sessions short in number of bursts and spread across separate occasions; stop at once on any discomfort.

**Mapping results to the plug-in (DESIGN).**

| Self-test or capture result | Plug-in parameter (`implementation.md` Key Question 6) | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Band tested (centre, Q used in the stimulus) | `centreHz`, `q` | The same filter in the stimulus and the plug-in |
| `T`: lowest "too hard" level, as band level | `ceilingDb` (absolute, dB re full-scale sine, before the slider) | `T` minus a margin of at least 5 dB (the retest spread); leave unset when "not reached within the cap" |
| Largest boost rated "fine" in test 2, as measured `L_band - L_wideband` | relative threshold `R` | Measured offline with the plug-in's detector; the Option A `bands.bin` layout has no field for `R` yet, and `implementation.md` line 205 says the code must carry both parameters |
| Excess of hurt-event frames over `R` (Key Question 4 capture) | `maxCutDb` | Cover the 90th-95th percentile of the excess and no more (extra cut risks dullness) |
| Hurt-event durations (Key Question 4 capture) | `attackMs`, `releaseMs`, `detectorMs` | No per-person source found; see `protection.md` Key Questions 4 and 6 and `implementation.md` Key Question 4 |

**New code needed (no estimates).** A generator (filtered noise, warble, clip playback with EQ), a C# K-weighting and loudness normaliser, a response dialog, session logging, telemetry gating, and later a loopback capture with band meters (Key Question 4).

## Key Question 4: How can the voices that bother Ariel be measured and compared with voices that do not (band energy over time, spectral centroid, DIN 45692 sharpness, specific loudness, a "that one hurt" capture)?

### Takeaway
The tray cannot see spectra today, so measurement needs a new loopback capture (shared mode) with a small filter bank. It can compute band energy over time, relative band shares, spectral centroid and the eGeMAPS alpha ratio and Hammarberg index live; sharpness (DIN 45692) and specific loudness need a Zwicker (ISO 532-1) or Moore-Glasberg (ISO 532-3) stage and are better done offline on saved clips. Compare hurt events with matched "fine" frames at the same K-weighted loudness, using level-independent features, because the complaint holds when volume is low.

### Cited Findings
- Loopback captures "the system mix that is being played by the audio engine"; it works only for shared-mode streams ("Exclusive-mode streams cannot operate in loopback mode"); when the hardware has no loopback pin, WASAPI copies the engine output into the capture buffer — [Microsoft: Loopback Recording](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/coreaudio/loopback-recording).
- The loopback page does not say where the tap sits relative to the APOs or the endpoint volume; the Microsoft Q&A that asks exactly this has no answer — [Microsoft Q&A 689393](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/689393/(loopback)capture-audio-are-apo-effects-captured-t); a forum thread reports mixed results — [GameDev.net 664976](https://www.gamedev.net/forums/topic/664976-rendering-audio-using-wasapi-loopback-capturing-the-speakers-volume/) (search summary).
- Endpoint volume "is applied to the post-mix audio stream, and functions as the master volume", implemented either by a volume APO inserted after the mix or by a hardware control — [Larry Osterman, Volume in Windows Vista part 2](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/larryosterman/volume-in-windows-vista-part-2-types-of-volume-in-windows-vista).
- Per-process loopback can include or exclude a process tree and needs Windows 10 build 20348 or later (Ariel's build is 10.0.26200) — [Microsoft Application Loopback sample](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/samples/microsoft/windows-classic-samples/applicationloopbackaudio-sample/).
- NAudio's `WasapiLoopbackCapture` sets the loopback stream flag, has a default constructor for the default render endpoint and one taking an `MMDevice` — [WasapiLoopbackCapture.cs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/naudio/NAudio/master/NAudio.Wasapi/WasapiLoopbackCapture.cs).
- Spectral centroid is "the weighted mean of the frequencies present in the signal", a magnitude-weighted average of the frequencies in a Fourier spectrum; the page says it has a "robust connection" to perceived brightness (citing Grey & Gordon 1978), and it does not discuss speech or frame-by-frame use — [Wikipedia: Spectral centroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_centroid).
- eGeMAPS voice descriptors: the alpha ratio compares energy in 50-1000 Hz with energy in 1-5 kHz; the Hammarberg index is the strongest peak below 2 kHz over the strongest peak in 2-5 kHz; spectral slopes are fitted over 0-500 Hz and 500-1500 Hz; definitions vary between studies — [eGeMAPS documentation](https://rdrr.io/github/humlab-speech/superassp/man/eGeMAPS.html) and [emvoice documentation](https://emvoice.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) (search summary).
- DIN 45692 sharpness in the MoSQITo code: `S = 0.11 * sum(N' * g * z * 0.1) / N` over a 0.1 Bark grid, with `g = 1` up to 15.8 Bark and `g = 0.15*exp(0.42*(z-15.8)) + 0.85` above; the input is specific loudness `N'`; `S` is set to 0 where `N` is below 0.1 sone — [MoSQITo `sharpness_din_from_loudness.py`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Eomys/MoSQITo/master/mosqito/sq_metrics/sharpness/sharpness_din/sharpness_din_from_loudness.py); contradicted in detail by a calculator's documentation that puts the break at 16 Bark and grows the weight as `0.066*exp(0.171*z)`, and says the 0.11 constant normalises a 1 kHz, 60 dB narrowband-noise reference to 1 acum — [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/sharpness-acum-din45692/) (search summary). The DIN text itself was not read.
- Specific loudness comes from the Zwicker method of ISO 532-1:2017 and the Moore-Glasberg-Schlittenlacher method of ISO 532-3:2023, which covers steady and time-varying sounds heard by adults with normal hearing and does not cover reducing a time-varying signal longer than 5 s to one figure; ISO's listing marks the 2023 edition as due for revision, with a second-edition committee draft (ISO/CD 532-3), and I did not check whether 532-1 has one — [ISO 532-1](https://www.iso.org/standard/63077.html), [ISO 532-3](https://www.iso.org/standard/69856.html) (search summary; fetch returned 403).
- In one-third-octave bands the gap between short-term and long-term speech levels is roughly 10 dB and steady across twelve languages and frequencies; the excerpts did not report per-band male versus female values — [Byrne et al. 1994, JASA 96:2108-2120](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/an-international-comparison-of-long-term-average-speech-spectra/) (search summary).
- Adult male F0 is about 90-155 Hz and adult female about 165-255 Hz; telephone speech covers about 300-3400 Hz — [Wikipedia: Voice frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice_frequency).
- Speech Intelligibility Index band-importance weights typically peak near 2 kHz — [SII package documentation](https://rdrr.io/cran/SII/f/inst/doc/SII.pdf) (search summary; the statement came from the result set, not a page I opened).
- LKFS and LUFS are synonyms (LUFS introduced in EBU R 128); both are absolute dB scales and LU describes differences — [Wikipedia: LKFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LKFS). The K-weighting numbers in use are in `native\dsp\SpDsp.h` lines 151-179 (Key Question 3).

### Inferences
- **Capture layer (DESIGN).** A loopback capture of the watched device in shared mode, a filter bank built with `BiQuadFilter`, a 400 ms K-weighted loudness like the Leveler's (so frames can be matched on loudness), and per-hop (for example 100 ms) features: levels in the plug-in's candidate bands (in its `dB re full-scale sine` unit) plus a few extra analysis bands, each band's level minus the K-weighted overall level (a level-independent share), the 2-5 kHz and 100-400 Hz shares, spectral centroid, alpha ratio and Hammarberg index. Sharpness and specific loudness: offline on saved clips with MoSQITo or similar (installing anything is a PC change and needs Ariel's own go-ahead).
- **Why several features (DESIGN).** The centroid is one number: a high voice with strong low harmonics and a deep voice with strong 3 kHz energy can share a centroid. DIN sharpness is normalised by loudness and its weighting only exceeds 1 above 15.8 Bark (about 3 kHz per `cause.md` Key Question 3), so it cannot see boom. Use band shares for both ends; use centroid and sharpness as summaries. The two sharpness weightings differ by about 17% at 18 Bark (1.23 versus 1.43), are close at 22 Bark (2.88 versus 2.84) and differ by about 39% at 24 Bark (5.55 versus 4.00) (DERIVED from the two formulas), so compare values from one tool only.
- **"That one hurt" button (DESIGN).** The tray keeps a ring buffer of the last 10-30 s of feature frames (frames are small, so the ring needs little memory), because the sound that hurt came before the press, and it keeps a few seconds after the press as well. A press stores: UTC time, active apps (`AppLevel.Name`), slider and ear level, cap, plug-in telemetry (Leveler `inputDb` and gain reduction), an optional short voice label, and the feature frames. Audio is not saved by default, because call audio contains other people's voices; whether saving it is acceptable was not researched.
- **Matched controls (DESIGN).** A "this one is fine" button, or automatic baseline frames from the same app and session, taken at K-weighted loudness within a few dB of the hurt frames, with no press within 30 s. Compare each level-independent feature (hurt versus fine): difference of medians with a bootstrap interval and the share of hurt frames above the 90th percentile of fine frames. With few events treat this as exploratory, split by source (Zoom versus YouTube) and by speaker label, and confirm by replaying a hurt clip with the suspect band lowered (test 2, Key Question 3).
- **Where the tap sits matters (UNCERTAIN).** If loopback is taken after the plug-in and the slider, the capture shows what the listener hears, not the source. Settle it with a fixed-tone experiment on the real chain: play a known tone, then toggle the plug-in and change the slider, and compare captured levels (the Microsoft Q&A suggests the same test). Both a before and an after view are needed for verification; if loopback gives only one, produce the other by running the saved source clips through the band stage offline (the native DSP tests described in `implementation.md` Key Question 7 already run the stage on buffers, so the same code could render clips; an inference, not a described feature).
- **Speech levels (DESIGN).** The Byrne gap means a band's short-term peaks run about 10 dB over its long-term level, so record both a fast and a slow band level.

## Key Question 5: How should the protection be verified (objective and subjective), and what should "success" mean?

### Takeaway
Verify in three layers: signal behaviour (band levels and gain reduction against the ceilings, no new clipping), blind listening (ABX for transparency, preference A/B on hurt clips, MUSHRA-style ratings with low-pass anchors) and field use (N-of-1 blocks counting "that one hurt" presses). Fix the success criteria before testing: fewer hurt events and a preference for the protected version on the bothersome voices, undetectable or at least not worse on everything else, band levels held to the ceilings, with calibration uncertain by about 5-6 dB.

### Cited Findings
- Test-retest spread of discomfort and category levels is about 5-6 dB — [Cox et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1097/00003446-199710000-00004) (6 dB or less), [Hawley et al. 2017](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28286362) (about 5 dB) and [Purdue AAI9622820](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI9622820) (within +-5 dB); abstracts and search summaries only, see Key Question 1.
- MUSHRA is a rating method with a hidden reference and anchors (7 kHz and 3.5 kHz low-pass), scored 0-100; a post-screening rule excludes listeners who rate the hidden reference below 90 on more than 15% of items — [Wikipedia: MUSHRA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MUSHRA); the 15% rule appears only in secondary pages and the current ITU text was not retrieved — [ITU-R BS.1534-0](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1534-0-200106-S!!MSW-E.doc) (search summary: a rejection step may be justified before or after a test, and the text asks for listeners with critical-listening experience and normal hearing).
- An N-of-1 study uses randomised treatment order with multiple crossovers and optional washout, suits reversible interventions, is limited by carry-over, and its power depends on the number of observations — [GOV.UK: N-of-1 study](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/n-of-1-study-comparative-studies) (16 July 2020).
- COSI (Client Oriented Scale of Improvement) uses up to five listening situations, each rated for degree of change and final ability — [NAL COSI questionnaire](https://www.nal.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/COSI-Questionnaire.pdf) (search summary; the PDF could not be fetched).
- Hearing-aid fittings are verified against targets by real-ear measurement ("we verify that with a real-ear measurement and adjust the amplification to match the prescription") — [Keidser & Dillon 2012](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/siemens-expert-series-nal-nl2-11355); the DSL maximum-output check uses a narrowband signal at 85 dB SPL (real ear) or 90 dB SPL (coupler) — [Scollie 2007](https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/dsl-version-v5-0-description-959).
- Telemetry available now: per slot `inputDb`, `gainReductionDb`, `maxGainReductionDb`, `outputPeakDb` and `clipperHits`, all broadband — `native\apo\SpShared.h` lines 102-108, `src\SoundProtection.Core\ApoTelemetry.cs` lines 46-58.
- Loopback is a shared-mode capture of the engine's mix, with an unresolved tap point (Key Question 4) — [Microsoft: Loopback Recording](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/coreaudio/loopback-recording).

### Inferences
**Objective layer (DESIGN).**
- *Tone ladder with existing telemetry.* For one band, play a tone at the band centre at ceiling -10, -5, 0, +5, +10 dB (never above the cap, Key Question 3). For a single tone, broadband equals band level, so the Safety Cap slot's `inputDb` against `outputPeakDb`, and the gain reduction, show whether the stage leaves it alone below the ceiling and holds it near the ceiling (within `maxCutDb`) above; if the telemetry input tap sits after the band stage, compare `outputPeakDb` with the level that was played instead. No loopback needed; this is the in-situ counterpart of the native test H18 (`implementation.md` Key Question 7).
- *Voices.* For replayable content (a saved video or file), capture the loopback once with the band stage off and once on (the stage is off when `bands.bin` is absent, `implementation.md` Key Question 6) and compare band levels over time (informative only if the loopback tap sits after the plug-in, Key Question 4); for live calls rely on per-band gain-reduction telemetry (the proposed `bandGrCentiDb[4]` in bytes 56..63 of the Safety Cap slot) and log, per band, the share of time with reduction above 0.5 dB, the mean and the maximum.
- *Side effects.* On fine content the broadband loudness is unchanged by the band stage within a stated tolerance, `clipperHits` does not rise, and gain reduction never exceeds `maxCutDb`.
- *Diagnosing from the logs.* A band reduced almost all the time by a similar amount on every voice is a static problem (headphone or ear), which a static EQ handles better (`protection.md` Key Questions 1 and 7). Hurt presses with no gain reduction mean the wrong band, ceiling or relative threshold. Large reduction and still hurting means `maxCutDb` too small or Q too narrow. Reports of muffled sound mean too much cut or too many bands; audible pumping means release too short.

**Subjective layer (DESIGN).**
- *Transparency (ABX, fine clips).* If the protected and unprotected versions of non-bothersome clips cannot be told apart, the stage is transparent there. "Not significant" is not proof, so state the number of trials and the power.
- *Preference (2AFC, hurt clips).* Which of two versions is easier to listen to, order randomised, protected side counterbalanced.
- *Clarity cost (MUSHRA-style).* Rate the unprocessed hidden reference, the protected clip, and 7 kHz and 3.5 kHz low-pass anchors on 0-100; if the protected clip rates near the anchors it removes too much (UNCERTAIN: MUSHRA was designed for audio-quality impairments, and its fit to tonal-balance changes was not checked).
- *Field (N-of-1).* Randomised blocks (one call or one viewing session each) with the band stage on or off, switched by the tray through `bands.bin` and not shown until the end; outcome: "that one hurt" presses per block, plus a COSI-style rating of up to five named situations.

**What "success" should mean (DESIGN; set before testing).**
1. Efficacy on bothersome voices: "that one hurt" presses per block fall with the stage on, and in blind 2AFC the protected version is chosen at or above the binomial threshold.
2. Transparency elsewhere: ABX on fine clips stays below the threshold, and MUSHRA-style ratings of the protected clips stay close to the hidden reference and well above the anchors, within a margin chosen in advance.
3. Technical: band levels sit at or under the ceilings (soft knee tolerance as in `implementation.md`), gain reduction never exceeds `maxCutDb`, the Safety Cap does not clip more, and broadband loudness on fine content is unchanged.
4. Stability: re-running the self-test on another occasion gives ceilings within 5-6 dB; a change of that size is noise, so a ceiling edit smaller than that cannot be validated by listening.
5. Not success: zero hurt events, a voice that sounds identical, or a stage that is always engaged.
6. Re-verify after a headphone change, a Realtek driver or effects change, a Windows update (the install report's live check found the Safety Cap still last in the effect list after install, `docs\03\INSTALL-REPORT.md` line 74, and its deferred-issues list says that order is neither checked nor repaired, line 313), or a change in usual slider position.

### Gaps
- No published norm for what a "too hard" tolerance or a successful per-band protection looks like; the criteria above are design choices.
- The BS.1534-3 post-screening rule rests on secondary pages; MUSHRA's fit to this use is unchecked.
- COSI response anchors were not read (the NAL PDF could not be fetched), and the AHRQ N-of-1 user's guide (2014) was seen only in search results.
- The smallest audible change in a speech band level (just-noticeable difference) was not sourced, so the tolerance for "unchanged" content is a choice.
- Whether switching the band stage on and off blind through `bands.bin` is practical (file write, plug-in pickup, ramps) is a plug-in question for `implementation.md`; the loopback tap point is unresolved.

## Key Question 6: Safety: this is not a medical device. When does sound sensitivity call for an audiologist (a sudden change, pain, tinnitus, one ear only)?

### Takeaway
Treat any sudden change in hearing as a medical emergency; see a GP for everyday sounds that feel too loud or painful; and expect a hearing examination for tinnitus that is one-sided, lasts 6 months or longer, or comes with hearing difficulty. The self-test must stop at the first "too hard", never exceed the cap, and make no claim about Ariel's hearing; a software test on consumer headphones cannot say whether sensitivity is abnormal.

### Cited Findings
- Sudden deafness: "you should consider sudden deafness symptoms a medical emergency and visit a doctor immediately"; it "frequently affects only one ear", arrives "all at once or over a few days", and treatment "delayed for more than two to four weeks is less likely to reverse or reduce permanent hearing loss"; associated symptoms include ear fullness, dizziness and ringing — [NIDCD: Sudden deafness](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/sudden-deafness) (last updated 14 Sept 2018).
- Hyperacusis ("everyday sounds seem much louder to you than they should") "can sometimes be painful"; the page says "See a GP if you think you have hyperacusis" (labelled non-urgent), which may lead to a hearing specialist; if sounds hurt or feel too loud during sound therapy "you should stop and contact your doctor for advice"; constant earplug or earmuff use is discouraged because it "could make you more sensitive to noise", and "do not avoid noise completely" — [NHS: Hyperacusis](https://tnaqa.mirrorweb.com/ukgwa/20260505142406mp_/https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hyperacusis/) (archive copy; page last reviewed 11 May 2026).
- Tinnitus guideline: for tinnitus that is one-sided, lasts 6 months or longer, or comes with hearing difficulties, clinicians should obtain a "prompt, comprehensive audiologic examination"; a baseline hearing test is optional for anyone with tinnitus — [AAO-HNSF Clinical practice guideline: tinnitus, Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg 2014;151(2 Suppl):S1-S40, PMID 25273878](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25273878) (abstract only, read through Europe PMC).
- For tinnitus the NIDCD page says to see the primary care doctor first, then possibly an ENT and an audiologist, and notes imaging may be ordered "especially if your tinnitus pulsates"; it does not list one-ear tinnitus as a warning sign and does not cover hyperacusis; it advises "limiting your exposure to loud noise" — [NIDCD: Tinnitus](https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/tinnitus) (updated 1 May 2023).
- UCSF EARS pages advise immediate medical contact for sudden hearing change (not waiting to see whether it improves), treat sudden loss developing over hours to three days with new severe vertigo or neurologic symptoms as urgent, and advise same-day contact for severe ear pain with fever, fluid leakage or swelling; the same search result set adds, without naming the page, that ear pain can occur at volumes below those usually considered hazardous, that pain alone cannot show whether damage occurred, and that a normal audiogram does not exclude sound-related pain — [UCSF EARS: Hyperacusis and sound sensitivity](https://ears.ucsf.edu/node/2651) and [UCSF EARS: Sound sensitivity](https://ears.ucsf.edu/node/2731) (search summary; both fetches returned 403).
- A hospital-group health-library page says one-sided presentations, intense ear pain and abrupt hearing changes warrant prompt medical care — [Acibadem hyperacusis page](https://acibademinternational.com/health-library/hyperacusis-what-patients-need-to-know/) (search summary; weak source).
- Standard audiometric starting levels can exceed a sensitive person's ULL (21% and 24% of 362 patients at different steps) — [Aazh & Moore 2017](https://doi.org/10.1044/2017_AJA-17-0011) (abstract only; Key Question 1).
- Vendors of consumer tests disclaim medical use: Mimi "does not constitute medical advice or diagnosis" — [Mimi blog](https://mimi.io/blog/how-to-interpret-your-hearing-test-results-mt-test-for-sound-personalization) (vendor page) — and SoundID "does not replace medical treatment for hearing ailments" — [Sonarworks blog](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/research/soundid-personalization-tailor-your-sound-and-stay-on-top-of-your-hearing-health) (vendor page).

### Inferences
- **Stop and see a clinician (DESIGN, built on the cited pages; not medical advice).** Any sudden change in hearing, new tinnitus or fullness, pain from sound, hard or loud sounds at very low levels across most bands, or a trend toward more bands or voices becoming "too hard" across sessions. Tinnitus that is one-sided, long-lasting or with hearing difficulty calls for an audiologic examination (AAO-HNSF). The Khalfa questionnaire could be offered only as an informational prompt, never scored as a diagnosis.
- **One ear only.** The sources give one-sided tinnitus (AAO-HNSF) as a reason for a prompt audiologic examination, and note that sudden deafness frequently affects one ear (NIDCD); the NHS and NIDCD tinnitus pages do not single out one-sided sensitivity, so no firm rule is sourced for it. An optional one-ear-at-a-time pass in the self-test (with the one-ear loudness caveat, Key Question 3) would show a clear difference between ears; treat a clear asymmetry as a reason to ask a clinician, not as a result.
- **The self-test cannot say whether sensitivity is abnormal.** Its scale is dBFS through an unknown headphone, not dB HL, so the clinical criteria (a 77 dB HL average, a HQ cut-off) do not transfer. The report should say so plainly, and the app should describe results only as personal settings.
- **Over-protection is an open question (UNCERTAIN).** The NHS advice against constant earplug use and against avoiding noise completely concerns hyperacusis treatment; whether a stage that always trims some bands behaves like that is untested. Ariel's complaint ("sometimes", for particular voices) fits an intermittent cut, but it is worth a question to an audiologist if the complaint grows.
- **Design consequences (DESIGN).** Start quiet, stop at the first "too hard", never repeat a level that was too hard, never exceed the cap, keep sessions short and spread out, show a plain-language stop-and-ask-a-clinician note before the first run, and record nothing that could be read as a diagnosis.

### Gaps
- No source states a rule for one-sided sound sensitivity without tinnitus or hearing loss; no source gives a time frame for a non-urgent audiology visit.
- The UCSF statements and the Acibadem statement were read only through a search summary (403 on fetch for UCSF); which statement sits on which UCSF page is unverified.
- The NIDCD sudden-deafness page dates from 2018 and the AAO-HNSF guideline from 2014; newer guidance may exist and was not searched for.
- Whether a threshold trend in a software self-test is a meaningful warning sign is unsupported; the "growing sensitivity" trigger above is a precaution, not an evidence-based criterion.
- Clinical validity of a headphone self-test is unknown; the Khalfa and Aazh & Moore criteria cannot be applied to it.
